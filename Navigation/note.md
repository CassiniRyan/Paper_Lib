# Navigation — Paper Notes

“Navigation” spans very different research questions in this folder: estimator degeneracy, map representations, semantic memory, social safety, embodied planning, and whole-body traversal. Each note explains the paper around its actual technical wager—what representation or decomposition it claims is missing—and comments on the evidence and failure modes that matter for that wager. Claims about very recent preprints should still be checked against the linked original paper.

## APREBot: Active Perception System for Reflexive Evasion Robot

APREBot targets the blind spots and latency of a single exteroceptive sensor during high-speed quadruped obstacle avoidance. It combines omnidirectional LiDAR surveillance with a steerable camera: LiDAR supplies the reflex trigger and coarse direction, while the camera actively turns toward the threat for richer local evidence. A hierarchical controller separates fast evasive reactions from deliberate perception and locomotion. The important idea is not merely sensor fusion, but allocating sensing effort according to risk and reaction time. Sim-to-real tests cover obstacles approaching from different directions. The architecture is attractive for safety layers, though its reflex behavior is narrower than general path planning and depends on reliable timing and calibration between sensors.

### System architecture, information flow, and assessment

The design is best understood as an attention-allocation system rather than a conventional learned navigation policy. A 360-degree LiDAR continuously supplies coarse range observations over the full azimuth. From these observations the first stage detects nearby objects, estimates their relative approach, and ranks them with a threat measure involving separation, approach direction, and time-to-collision. The highest-risk object becomes the active-perception target. A pan/tilt or otherwise steerable RGB-D camera is then directed toward that bearing, where higher-resolution image processing performs detection, tracking, segmentation, and depth-based pose refinement. The controller therefore always retains a fast LiDAR-level threat estimate while opportunistically replacing it with a richer camera-level estimate when the camera has acquired the target.

The input/output boundary is unusually clear. Inputs are the LiDAR point/range stream, RGB-D images, robot state, and target-relative motion estimates; the perception output is a prioritized obstacle state; the behavioral output is an evasive locomotion command passed to the quadruped controller. This is not an end-to-end RGB-to-joint-action network and is not principally an RL contribution. Learned 2-D vision components may appear inside the camera branch, but the paper's core is hierarchical sensor scheduling and reflex logic. That distinction matters: the system remains interpretable and can react before the narrow-field camera finishes slewing.

The highlight is the explicit treatment of sensing latency. Ordinary fusion assumes all sensors are already observing the same object, whereas APREBot asks which object deserves scarce high-resolution sensing now. This is a useful pattern for robots that need omnidirectional safety but cannot carry panoramic high-resolution depth cameras. Its weakest cases follow directly from the architecture: rapid target switching can cause camera thrashing; multiple simultaneous threats are compressed to one focus target; LiDAR association errors contaminate the camera cue; and calibration, timestamping, and actuator slew delay enter the safety loop. The experiments support reflexive evasion, not a complete global-navigation claim. A strong next step would couple multi-hypothesis threat tracking and reachable-set prediction to the gaze scheduler, report end-to-end latency margins, and expose a conservative fallback whenever camera acquisition fails.

### What closes the loop and what remains outside it

The fast loop can react from LiDAR before semantic classification is available; active RGB-D viewing then refines object extent and motion so evasion can be updated rather than executed as a fixed reflex. This ordering is safety-relevant: making the camera mandatory would add mechanical slew and inference delay to every response. Robot proprioception and locomotion phase must also enter the decision because the same obstacle bearing is not equally dangerous during stance, flight, or a fast turn. The final output is therefore a body- or velocity-level avoidance request, while a separate locomotion controller owns joint stabilization.

APREBot should be compared with panoramic collision avoidance on reaction time, missed threats, minimum clearance, unnecessary evasions, and recovery to the original route—not only detector accuracy. A useful ablation would freeze the camera, remove LiDAR prioritization, and vary camera slew rate and timestamp error. Multi-target tracking with an explicit risk budget could preserve several hypotheses while directing the camera toward the observation with greatest expected safety benefit. The architecture is most convincing as an independent reflex layer beneath a slower planner, with clearly specified arbitration when their commands disagree.

### Timing budget, threat estimation, and decisive experiments

For a reflex system, the relevant quantity is not sensor frame rate alone but the complete detection-to-motion budget: LiDAR scan accumulation, clustering and association, threat calculation, camera rotation, RGB-D inference, command arbitration, and the locomotion controller's physical response. Time-to-collision should be compared with this sum plus braking or lateral-motion time. Relative velocity uncertainty also matters: differentiating noisy ranges can make a stationary close object appear more threatening than a faster distant one. Filtering must remain causal and should expose confidence rather than silently smoothing away rapid approach.

The camera branch adds value when texture, object boundary, or denser depth changes the chosen maneuver. The paper would be clearer with a decision-level ablation: how often does focused RGB-D reverse or resize the LiDAR-only avoidance command, and does that improve clearance without increasing delay? Trials should stratify front, rear, and lateral attacks; small versus large objects; one versus several movers; camera already facing versus facing away; and stationary clutter during evasion. Reporting detection recall without these control consequences is insufficient.

My judgment is that active perception is the paper's durable idea, while the particular reflex logic is replaceable. A stronger version would formulate gaze as expected risk reduction, keep several tracked obstacle hypotheses, and choose camera motion jointly with a reachable-set controller. It should have a strictly LiDAR-based emergency action when visual refinement cannot arrive in time and a hysteretic return to planned navigation after the hazard passes. This would convert an effective demonstration into an auditable safety subsystem.

## ANYmal Parkour Learning Agile Navigation for Quadrupedal Robots

ANYmal Parkour learns to traverse obstacles that require distinct motor skills—walking, jumping, climbing, and crouching—while still progressing toward a navigation goal. Specialist policies are trained in simulation and distilled into a single perception-conditioned policy. Depth sensing and proprioception provide local terrain information; a high-level route or target guides progression. The main achievement is real-world, autonomous chaining of agile skills rather than executing hand-selected demonstrations. Its novelty is the scalable teacher/expert curriculum and unified student that selects behavior implicitly. It remains a local, learned navigation system: global semantic reasoning and guarantees outside the simulated terrain distribution are not its focus.

### Perception, skill hierarchy, and autonomous parkour

The deployed system is a hierarchy rather than a single distilled controller. Five low-level RL experts implement walking, jumping, climbing up, climbing down, and crouching. Above them, a learned navigation actor consumes the goal and a compact belief reconstructed from local 3-D observations. Its modified PPO action has a categorical component selecting the expert and a Gaussian component producing continuous position, heading, and timing parameters. This distinction is central: navigation learns both *which* contact-rich capability applies and *how* to command it, while each expert retains a focused training distribution and outputs 12-actuator joint commands.

ANYmal-D carries six RealSense depth cameras (front, rear, left, and right coverage) plus a Velodyne Puck LiDAR. Their point clouds feed a multi-resolution neural reconstruction model: fine geometry is preserved near the body for contact/clearance, while coarser distant structure gives greater look-ahead and retains overhangs that a height map would erase. Perception runs asynchronously on a Jetson Orin, so the policy consumes the latest available belief rather than an ideal synchronized scan. The map can remember an overhead surface after it leaves view and correct itself following localization jumps.

The result is autonomous chaining, not a scripted obstacle sequence. The robot reportedly reaches 2 m/s, crosses roughly 1-m gaps and platforms, traverses 40-degree slopes and 0.25-m stairs, and crouches through 0.4-m passages. On randomized layouts, learned navigation reaches approximately 96.3–98.2% success and can deliberately detour when an obstacle lies outside a skill's capability. Interesting reuse emerges: the jump expert can perform a rapid constrained turn because its training already demanded heading changes on small supports.

The modular design is highly inspectable but imposes a closed skill vocabulary; it cannot smoothly synthesize a behavior between two experts. Near-limit maneuvers also involve motor saturation, large joint excursions, impact, and reconstruction uncertainty that success rate alone does not characterize. Future work should attach uncertainty and feasibility margins to expert selection, log thermal/impact/intervention costs, add retreat and recovery skills, predict dynamic obstacles, and explore mixtures or compositional skill latents without losing the debugging advantage of explicit modules.

### Training decomposition and what the student actually learns

Each locomotion expert is trained with privileged terrain information and a curriculum specialized to one obstacle family. The navigation layer is not simply a classifier over obstacle labels: its continuous parameters specify where and how the selected expert should act, and PPO learns this decision from progress, collision, feasibility, and traversal outcomes. The perception network converts incomplete depth/LiDAR observations into a persistent multi-resolution terrain belief, so expert selection operates on inferred geometry rather than raw images. This separates three uncertainties—terrain reconstruction, skill applicability, and low-level execution—that an end-to-end policy would entangle.

The strongest evidence is chaining and recovery across unseen arrangements. A successful isolated jump says little about whether the robot can approach at the right speed, land with a useful heading, and immediately select a climb. Failure analysis should therefore be transition-conditioned: expert confusion, approach-parameter error, landing margin, map uncertainty, and success for each skill-to-skill pair. A future mixture could blend experts only in dynamically compatible regions while retaining a discrete fallback and feasibility critic. Adding moving-object prediction and a global route layer would extend the system without claiming that the current local terrain policy solves semantic or building-scale navigation.

### Skill feasibility, training distribution, and evidence quality

The high-level actor is learning an implicit capability model: a gap is not merely labeled “jump,” but evaluated relative to approach direction, available run-up, desired landing pose, and the expert's learned limits. Simulation terrain generation must therefore sample obstacle sequences and transitions, not only isolated blocks. Expert curricula expand obstacle height, gap, slope, and clearance as performance improves; the navigation policy then receives failures as evidence that a nominally visible route is outside the skill envelope. The reconstruction model is equally important because limited viewpoints occur precisely when the robot pitches, crouches, or places its body near an obstacle.

Ablations should distinguish perfect terrain, reconstructed terrain, individual experts, and the full selector. The gap between privileged and onboard perception measures mapping cost; oracle skill selection measures routing cost; per-expert execution measures motor cost. The reported real speeds and obstacle dimensions are compelling, but robust autonomy also requires repeated trials with shifted and visually altered obstacles, accumulated localization error, and recovery after a rejected maneuver. Energy, peak torque, body impact, and hardware intervention should accompany success.

The approach is a strong engineering choice when obstacle families are known and interpretable. Its main weakness is coverage: an obstacle requiring a partial climb followed by a jump may fit no expert, and categorical routing can chatter at boundaries. A learned feasibility score per expert, uncertainty-aware look-ahead over several selections, and a general recovery expert would reduce this brittleness. Longer-term, a compositional latent action space could interpolate skills while preserving named experts as safe anchors and diagnostic baselines.

## Collision-Free Humanoid Traversal in Cluttered Indoor Scenes

This work treats humanoid navigation as a whole-body collision problem rather than a point or circular-footprint problem. A planner/controller coordinates base progress, posture, arms, and torso so the robot can squeeze through narrow indoor geometry without striking obstacles. The design couples a geometric scene representation and collision-aware motion generation with a dynamically stable whole-body tracking controller. Its useful insight is that ordinary 2-D navigation clearance is insufficient for humanoids: upper-body configuration is part of the route. Results demonstrate more feasible traversal in constrained scenes. Limitations include dependence on scene geometry and the computational or modeling burden of whole-body collision checking.

### Humanoid potential field as observation and reward

HumanoidPF constructs an artificial potential field over the full articulated body. An attractive component uses obstacle-aware geodesic distance to the goal, while repulsive gradients come from a scene signed-distance representation. The field is sampled at 13 body locations, so a policy receives actionable directions for the head, shoulders, torso, knees, and other links instead of a dense occupancy volume. For training, desired direction and concentration at every link define a von Mises–Fisher distribution; the log likelihood of actual link velocity supplies a dense directional reward. Static priority favors root progress, while dynamic urgency rises when a moving link approaches collision, helping break the left/right cancellation common in naive potential fields.

Specialist PPO policies train on 139 cropped 5 x 5 m blocks from 3D-FRONT plus 216 procedural scenes containing hanging, floor-level, and tightly spaced obstacles. DAgger distills the scene-specific teachers into a generalist, with observation noise, force disturbances, and progressively reduced clearance. Deployment uses Fast-LIO2 localization, OctoMap geometry, and field rebuilding at 10 Hz; forward kinematics queries the field at current link positions and the policy emits whole-body targets after a user supplies a goal.

The strongest idea is the interface between mapping and RL. Rather than hoping a network discovers articulated collision gradients from raw voxels, the representation states which way each part should move. Experiments cover crouching, hurdling, sideways passages, and combined high/low clutter; comparisons indicate better robustness than multilayer-elevation and voxel observations under real sensing.

Potential fields remain local: minima, oscillation, and deadlock can persist, and 10-Hz mapping is inadequate for fast people or doors. Fast-LIO/OctoMap also assumes observable, mostly static geometry. Because all contact is treated as forbidden, the system cannot brace or use a rail. A global/topological guide, dynamic occupancy prediction, explicit minimum-clearance and latency metrics, and contact-affordance fields would turn this compelling local whole-body interface into a stronger general navigation system.

### Policy inputs, outputs, and the meaning of “collision free”

The generalist does not ingest raw RGB. At each control step it receives proprioception, goal information, and potential-field samples evaluated at current positions of selected body links. These vectors tell every link both attractive direction and nearby repulsive direction; the policy emits a coordinated whole-body target realized by a lower-level actuator interface. DAgger matters because the student visits configurations and clearances that scene-specific teachers did not, so querying teachers on student states reduces compounding collision error.

“Collision free” is empirical within the sensed static map and modeled robot geometry, not a continuous-time certificate. OctoMap discretization can erase thin obstacles, localization error shifts every body sample, and collision may occur between the 13 sampled sites. Stronger evaluation would report swept-volume minimum distance by link, collision impulse, false-free map rate, and success versus map/control latency. Adaptive sampling near predicted contact, uncertainty-inflated obstacles, and a short-horizon safety projection could preserve learned posture behavior while catching motions that the sampled potential field misses.

### Field construction, reward geometry, and missing guarantees

HumanoidPF uses scene signed distance and a goal-conditioned geodesic field to calculate a preferred motion direction at each sampled body point. The von Mises–Fisher reward is a particularly thoughtful choice: it rewards directional alignment on a sphere rather than treating a normalized direction as ordinary Euclidean regression. Dynamic priority increases the weight of a link that is close to or moving into an obstacle, while static root priority prevents many simultaneous avoidance vectors from destroying global progress. This representation supplies dense anticipatory supervision even before physical collision.

Hybrid scene generation serves two roles. Crops from 3D-FRONT provide realistic furniture arrangements and correlated clutter; procedural obstacles deliberately cover hard geometric cases such as low bars, narrow slots, and mixed upper/lower constraints. The comparison that matters is not simply hybrid versus procedural success, but held-out layouts, object shapes, clearance bands, and combinations absent from training. Oracle mesh, reconstructed OctoMap, and noisy localization should be evaluated separately to expose the representation's real sim-to-real gap.

The method's high point is converting articulated collision avoidance into a compact policy interface. However, a direction field does not guarantee that the next whole-body target has a collision-free swept volume or maintains dynamic balance. Thin obstacles between samples and rapid arm motion are obvious counterexamples. Adding continuous collision checking or a model-predictive projection after the policy would provide a firmer safety boundary. Deadlock detection should trigger backing up or global replanning rather than amplifying local gradients. Contact-affordance extensions could also distinguish forbidden furniture from surfaces that may safely support a hand.

## DCReg: Decoupled Characterization for Efficient Degenerate LiDAR Registration

DCReg addresses LiDAR registration when corridors, tunnels, planes, or limited fields of view make some motion directions weakly observable. It explicitly characterizes degeneracy and decouples well-constrained from poorly constrained components instead of trusting a single ill-conditioned pose solve. This allows robust updates in observable directions while regularizing or obtaining assistance for the rest. The work belongs primarily to localization, but it matters to navigation because planner maps and obstacle positions become unsafe when odometry silently drifts. Its strength is interpretable geometry-aware handling of degeneracy; performance still depends on enough useful structure or auxiliary information eventually becoming available.

### Schur-complement diagnosis and selective preconditioning

DCReg begins from the Hessian of point-cloud registration and asks why standard eigenvalue tests can misdiagnose degeneracy. Rotation and translation are coupled in the full six-DoF system, so a small eigenvalue does not cleanly reveal the physical unconstrained motion. A Schur-complement decomposition eliminates the coupling and produces separate rotational and translational subproblems. Eigenvectors in these clean spaces are then mapped back to explicit physical directions, identifying, for example, translation along a corridor or rotation insufficiently constrained by visible planes.

The same characterization drives mitigation. Instead of damping the entire normal equation—thereby degrading good measurements—DCReg constructs a preconditioner that stabilizes only detected weak directions and solves with preconditioned conjugate gradients. Its interface is therefore geometric rather than learned: input correspondences/Jacobians and residuals yield a conditioned six-DoF pose increment plus interpretable observability diagnostics. A single physically meaningful threshold controls intervention.

This detect–interpret–repair chain is the highlight. The paper reports 20–50% localization improvement and 5–100x speedups over compared degeneracy methods across varied environments, suggesting that principled linear algebra can outperform heavier heuristics. But regularization cannot create missing information: prolonged motion in a truly featureless tunnel still needs IMU, wheel/contact constraints, loop closure, or active sensing. Results also depend on correspondence quality and linearization; dynamic objects or bad associations can look like conditioning trouble. Future integration should expose directional covariance to planners, adapt sensor viewpoint to restore observability, and distinguish geometric degeneracy from outlier-induced Hessian corruption.

### Practical estimator interpretation

The output is more informative than a Boolean “degenerate” flag. Separated eigenvectors identify which rotational or translational directions are weak, while the conditioned solve still updates reliable axes. A downstream estimator can fuse IMU or wheel/contact priors only where LiDAR lacks evidence and avoid contaminating well-observed directions. This is particularly useful in corridors, open fields, and planar tunnels, where freezing the full six-DoF pose wastes valid information.

Threshold selection remains consequential: an aggressive threshold over-regularizes normal scenes, while a permissive one allows drift. The reported single threshold improves usability, but it should be calibrated against range noise, scan pattern, correspondence count, and scale. A robust deployment would combine the condition spectrum with residual-consistency tests so moving people or failed associations are treated as outliers rather than missing geometry. Planners should receive anisotropic covariance and avoid maneuvers whose safety depends on an unobservable axis; an active platform could move specifically to increase the weakest eigenvalue.

Long-duration tests should additionally report drift resolved along each diagnosed weak direction, showing whether the characterization predicts the physical error that actually accumulates.

### Mathematical scope, baselines, and deployment consequences

Schur complementation is not only a computational device here. Eliminating one variable block produces conditional rotational and translational information matrices in which eigenvectors correspond more cleanly to physical motion. DCReg then maps a weak eigendirection back to the original six-dimensional increment and preconditions only that subspace. Preconditioned conjugate gradient avoids a costly dense decomposition or generic damping at every iteration. The one interpretable parameter governs when a direction is treated as insufficiently constrained.

The headline 20–50% accuracy improvement and 5–100× speed advantage should be read with the evaluated registration backend, point association, hardware, and degeneracy distribution in mind. A complete benchmark should include long corridors, tunnels, single planes, open fields, changing scan FoV, and transitions back to rich geometry; report detection precision/recall for each weak degree of freedom; and compare runtime at matched convergence tolerance. Artificial Hessians test the algebra, while real trajectories reveal correspondence and linearization failures.

DCReg solves ill-conditioned optimization, not global localization. Its targeted prior may prevent an explosive increment but can still accumulate drift along a truly unobserved axis. The most useful systems integration is to export weak directions and conditioned covariance to a multisensor estimator. IMU, wheel or leg odometry, GNSS, loop closure, or deliberate viewpoint motion can then contribute where LiDAR cannot. A robust estimator should also distinguish low information from inconsistent information: a well-conditioned Hessian built from moving objects or wrong correspondences can be confidently incorrect.

Publishing each detected weak direction and applied preconditioning strength would also make field interventions reproducible and diagnosable rather than hiding them inside final pose accuracy.

## Elevation Mapping for Locomotion and Navigation using GPU

This system builds robot-centric elevation maps fast enough for online legged locomotion and planning. Range measurements are projected into a grid whose cells maintain height and uncertainty; GPU parallelism accelerates fusion, ray processing, filtering, and map updates. The output is a compact 2.5-D terrain representation suitable for traversability scoring, footstep planning, or learned policies. The key achievement is practical high-rate mapping on onboard hardware. Compared with learned perception, it is transparent and reusable, but overhangs and multiple surfaces in one cell are difficult to represent, and map quality remains tied to state-estimation and sensor calibration.

### GPU mapping pipeline and traversability output

The system receives registered point clouds plus robot pose and pose uncertainty, transforms samples into a rolling robot-centered grid, and updates per-cell elevation mean/variance. CUDA/CuPy parallelization covers point association, uncertainty-weighted fusion, map shifting, ray/visibility cleanup, and post-processing. Height-drift compensation prevents state-estimator vertical drift from leaving duplicated terrain; an exclusion zone and ray casting remove robot/self and stale overhang artifacts. Plugins generate smoothed/inpainted height, surface normal, traversability, or planar-region layers for consumers.

A learned traversability filter operates on local geometric patches, but the mapper itself is not an end-to-end policy. Its outputs are explicit grid layers used by foothold optimizers, local planners, or RL actors; no joint action or fixed history window is inherent. This boundary is a strength: the same map can support several controllers, be visualized, and carry uncertainty rather than hiding terrain inside a policy latent.

The contribution is primarily systems throughput. Elevation fusion is embarrassingly parallel but traditional CPU implementations struggle with dense modern LiDARs and expensive visibility updates; GPU execution makes richer cleanup/filtering practical at locomotion rates. Yet one height distribution per `(x,y)` cannot represent both floor and ceiling, vertical walls are awkward, and a falsely low variance can be more dangerous than an obvious hole. Calibration/pose error smears obstacles, while inpainting may invent support. Multi-layer or voxel fallbacks, age/confidence channels, dynamic-object rejection, and risk-weighted evaluation of false free space are more valuable next steps than optimizing height RMSE alone.

### Consumer contract and safety-relevant failure modes

The mapper's contract is a time-stamped tensor of elevation, variance, validity or age, normals, and derived traversability in a rolling local frame. A footstep planner can query support height and slope, while a learned locomotion policy consumes fixed-size patches; neither should treat an inpainted cell as measured ground. Pose covariance should enlarge height uncertainty during fast motion, and map shifting must preserve world consistency without copying stale terrain into newly exposed cells.

GPU acceleration matters only if transfer and synchronization are controlled. Sensor-to-GPU copies, pose arrival, asynchronous kernels, and planner reads can otherwise create a high-rate but old map. Evaluation should report end-to-end measurement age, jitter, dropped points, and false-positive/negative traversability in addition to kernel speed. A useful extension is an explicit representation switch: ordinary floor remains 2.5-D, but cells with multiple returns, ceilings, or vertical faces promote to layers or local voxels. Dynamic points should decay quickly, whereas static support should retain evidence with uncertainty growth.

### Fusion equations, plugins, and reproducibility questions

Each incoming point is transformed using the estimated sensor pose and assigned to a grid cell. Measurement variance accounts for range noise and pose uncertainty; Bayesian-style fusion updates the cell elevation and variance rather than overwriting height. The rolling map shifts with the robot, and visibility cleanup uses rays to remove stale surfaces that should have become free. Height drift compensation adjusts the map when the estimator's vertical reference changes. Post-processing plugins can inpaint holes, smooth noise, estimate normals, segment planes, or infer traversability without hard-wiring one locomotion task into the core mapper.

The GPU contribution includes data layout and parallel operations, not just “running code on CUDA.” Point insertion, cellwise fusion, shifting, ray traversal, and filters have different memory-access patterns and synchronization costs. Meaningful performance reporting should give grid resolution and extent, points per scan, sensor rate, map latency and jitter, GPU model, CPU/GPU transfer, and plugin configuration. Comparisons at different resolutions or with visibility cleanup disabled are not equivalent. The DARPA Subterranean and quadruped deployments are valuable evidence that the implementation survives real workloads.

For control, false free space is more consequential than mean height error. A small obstacle erased by ray cleanup or a hole filled as support can cause a fall, whereas a false obstacle mainly causes a detour. Evaluation should therefore be risk-weighted and distinguish measured, inferred, stale, and unknown cells. My preferred extension is a hybrid map with per-cell surface hypotheses and provenance: retain the efficient ground layer, but promote ambiguous vertical or multi-return regions to local multilayer/voxel storage. Consumers could then select speed and footholds based on uncertainty instead of receiving a deceptively complete height image.

## Elevator-LIO: Robust LiDAR-Inertial Odometry for Multi-Floor Navigation under Elevator-Induced Non-Inertial Motion

Elevator-LIO targets a failure case of conventional LiDAR-inertial odometry: elevator acceleration violates assumptions used to infer gravity and vertical motion, while the enclosed cabin has repetitive, weak geometry. The system detects or models elevator motion and adjusts inertial/LiDAR fusion so pose remains consistent across floors. This enables continuous multi-floor localization rather than resetting after a lift ride. The novel contribution is treating elevators as a distinct non-inertial regime inside the estimator. It is highly relevant for building-scale service robots, although detection errors and unusual elevators can still challenge the regime logic.

### Mode-dependent error-state filtering

Ordinary LIO interprets accelerometer readings in an inertial world frame. Inside an accelerating cabin, however, apparent vertical force mixes gravity, elevator acceleration, and robot motion while LiDAR sees a small reflective box. Elevator-LIO factorizes state into robot motion relative to the cabin and the cabin's own vertical motion, then embeds those variables in a mode-dependent iterated error-state Kalman filter. In normal indoor mode it reduces to conventional LIO; in elevator mode it propagates and constrains elevator-related states without falsely assigning cabin acceleration to robot pose.

An event manager detects entry, motion, stops, and exit from LiDAR range statistics and estimated state. At stops, event-triggered zero-velocity and zero-acceleration updates suppress accumulated vertical drift. Adaptive voxel downsampling maintains a stable number of useful points as the sensor transitions between a confined car and a large lobby. Inputs remain time-aligned LiDAR scans and IMU measurements; outputs are continuous pose, velocity, biases, gravity/cabin-motion estimates, and mode, rather than a learned navigation action.

Evaluation spans 20 real sequences and 79 rides with pedestrians, mirrors, large lobbies, and long vertical travel; 17 sequences finish below 1-cm height error, while standard-indoor tests remain competitive. The key insight is not treating elevator corruption as generic noise but changing the state model when the reference frame itself accelerates. The main fragility is mode recognition: a false transition applies the wrong process/measurement constraints, and unusual lifts, robot motion within the car, or prolonged door occlusion may violate event assumptions. Joint semantic/elevator detection, multiple-model probabilistic switching, explicit mode confidence, and building-map floor constraints would make the estimator safer for unattended deployment.

### Observability during an elevator trip

Four phases provide different information. Entry and exit expose rich lobby geometry; door closing creates a confined cabin map; vertical travel produces apparent acceleration but little external geometry; stops provide near-zero relative motion. Elevator-LIO exploits those changes instead of expecting one stationary-world model to cover all of them. IMU propagates high-rate orientation, velocity, and bias, LiDAR constrains pose relative to cabin surfaces, and event constraints correct cabin velocity or acceleration at stops. The desired output is a continuous building-frame trajectory whose vertical displacement agrees with the floor transition.

Centimeter terminal height is valuable, but navigation also needs correct transient pose so the robot does not move unsafely near doors or passengers. Benchmarks should vary ride duration, acceleration profile, cabin size/material, robot motion inside the car, door occlusion, and false mode switches. An interacting-multiple-model filter could maintain normal and elevator hypotheses until evidence resolves them, while barometric pressure, floor maps, door semantics, or elevator telemetry provide independent cues. The planner should receive mode confidence and restrict motion whenever vertical state remains ambiguous.

### State transition logic, error modes, and building-level integration

Elevator-LIO's event manager is effectively a hybrid-state estimator. Entry changes which geometry is assumed static; detected cabin acceleration activates the non-inertial model; a stop supplies zero-motion constraints; exit restores the building frame. Robot motion relative to the cabin must remain distinguishable from cabin motion relative to the building. The filter therefore needs correct timestamps and uncertainty through every transition, not merely a post-hoc floor-height correction. Adaptive voxel sampling also avoids overwhelming registration with many redundant wall points inside a small car while preserving features in an open lobby.

The reported 79 rides and 20 sequences—with 17 ending below one-centimeter height error—show impressive terminal consistency. Still, terminal height can hide transient error that would corrupt a door-crossing map. Useful plots include vertical position, velocity, apparent gravity, bias, innovation, and mode probability throughout acceleration and braking. Baselines should be evaluated both with and without robot movement in the cabin, passengers, reflective walls, doors opening mid-estimation, and rides that pause or reverse.

For actual multi-floor navigation, continuous odometry must be connected to discrete building semantics. Elevator identity, entry side, selected floor, door state, and lobby relocalization can constrain the estimate and detect an impossible transition. A probabilistic multiple-model approach would avoid a brittle instantaneous mode switch. If confidence is low, the navigation policy should stop inside the cabin or at the threshold rather than treating a smooth pose as certain. This explicit estimator–planner contract is the main step from an excellent odometry specialization to dependable service-robot operation.

## FAST-LIO: A Fast, Robust LiDAR-inertial Odometry Package by Tightly-Coupled Iterated Kalman Filter

FAST-LIO provides low-latency, high-rate odometry by tightly coupling raw LiDAR points with IMU measurements in an iterated error-state Kalman filter. It directly registers points to the maintained map and jointly updates pose, velocity, biases, and other states, avoiding a slow feature-extraction front end. The second-generation implementation adds an efficient incremental spatial data structure. Its achievement is robust real-time estimation across several LiDAR types and aggressive motion, making it a common localization backbone. It supplies metric state rather than navigation decisions; severe geometric degeneracy, bad synchronization, or poorly calibrated sensors can still cause failure.

### Iterated filter, measurement scaling, and FAST-LIO2 map

IMU measurements propagate a nominal state containing rotation, position, velocity, gravity, and inertial biases (with LiDAR–IMU extrinsics where estimated). Deskewed LiDAR points are associated with local map planes; point-to-plane residuals enter an iterated error-state Kalman update, repeatedly relinearized on the manifold. The original FAST-LIO derives a Kalman-gain form whose dominant cost depends on small state dimension rather than thousands of point measurements, enabling more than 1,200 effective points and a full iterative update in under 25 ms on reported quadrotor hardware.

FAST-LIO2 removes handcrafted edge/plane feature extraction and registers raw points directly. That improves compatibility with nonrepetitive solid-state and spinning sensors and retains subtle geometry. Its ikd-Tree supports incremental insertion, deletion, downsampling, and rebalancing, allowing the local map to evolve without periodic reconstruction; reported operation reaches up to 100 Hz and survives rotations near 1,000 degrees/s in tested cases. Inputs are timestamped IMU and LiDAR packets, and outputs are odometric state/covariance plus an incrementally updated point map—not semantic perception, route choice, or control.

Tight coupling makes every LiDAR residual inform inertial state and allows IMU dynamics to carry short geometric gaps. The compact update and data structure explain the package's broad reuse better than simply calling it “fast.” Nevertheless, it is odometry without inherent loop closure, and observability still collapses in long planar/corridor motion. Timing/extrinsic errors, vibration, moving points, and incorrect planar associations can cause confident drift. Online temporal calibration, degeneracy-aware covariance, dynamic filtering, loop closure, and explicit health signals to the planner are essential around this excellent local estimator.

### State, output rate, and relationship to navigation

FAST-LIO is a state estimator, not an obstacle avoider. Its input is timestamped IMU plus raw LiDAR points; its state contains pose, velocity, gravity, inertial biases, and optionally LiDAR–IMU extrinsics. Its output is high-rate odometry and an incrementally maintained geometric map. A planner uses that pose to register obstacles and goals, but FAST-LIO does not decide traversability, choose a route, or output velocity or joint commands.

Tight coupling is valuable because accepted point residuals directly correct the inertial state, and IMU propagation supplies the motion needed to deskew a scan collected over time. Iteration relinearizes after correction, important under aggressive rotation. Still, locally smooth odometry can drift globally. Deployment should monitor innovations, correspondence geometry, time offset, and directional covariance; down-weight dynamic clusters; and trigger relocalization when consistency collapses. Navigation evaluations should distinguish odometry failures from mapping and control errors instead of reporting only task success.

### Algorithmic path from measurements to pose, and fair interpretation

At IMU rate, nonlinear dynamics propagate orientation on the rotation manifold, position, velocity, gravity, and bias covariance. Because one LiDAR scan is acquired over time, this trajectory deskews every point to a common reference. At scan update, nearest map neighbors define local planes and point-to-plane residuals. The iterated error-state filter solves for a small state correction, retracts it onto the manifold, and repeats until convergence. FAST-LIO2's incremental `ikd-Tree` supports insertion, deletion, nearest-neighbor search, and a bounded local map without rebuilding a conventional k-d tree each frame.

The efficient gain derivation exploits state dimension being far smaller than measurement count; this explains why thousands of raw points can be used without a measurement-sized matrix inversion. Removing hand-crafted edge/plane feature extraction in FAST-LIO2 broadens compatibility with spinning and solid-state LiDAR, but it also makes robust association and map maintenance decisive. Reported high rates should always be paired with point count, update iterations, processor, and mapping load.

Accuracy comparisons must distinguish odometry, mapping, and loop closure. FAST-LIO's core provides locally consistent motion; it does not guarantee a globally closed trajectory or semantic map. Aggressive motion tests deskewing and inertial coupling, whereas a long featureless corridor tests observability. Online diagnostics should include innovation whiteness, weak Hessian directions, bias growth, map correspondence ratio, and timing residual. When those degrade, the system should reduce speed, seek geometric excitation, or invoke another localization source. This operational response is more valuable to navigation than reporting one average trajectory error.

Default noise parameters and convergence thresholds should be reported separately for spinning and non-repetitive solid-state scan patterns, since their geometric conditioning differs substantially.

## FSR-VLN: Fast and Slow Reasoning for Vision-Language Navigation with Hierarchical Multi-modal Scene Graph

[FSR-VLN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.13733) reduces the latency and weak long-range reasoning of VLM-only navigation. It stores an environment as a hierarchical multimodal scene graph spanning rooms, views, and objects. Fast embedding-based retrieval narrows the candidates; expensive VLM reasoning is invoked only when ambiguity remains. This coarse-to-fine “fast then slow” design preserves visual evidence and supports language queries. The paper reports higher retrieval success on humanoid-collected indoor data and substantially less response time than reasoning over tour videos. It is an effective semantic goal selector, but still relies on separate mapping, planning, and control modules and on the quality of the prebuilt graph.

### Hierarchical retrieval and conditional slow reasoning

The Hierarchical Multi-modal Scene Graph stores room-level context, representative viewpoints, and object instances together with images/textual or embedding features and their spatial links. A natural-language instruction is resolved progressively: inexpensive similarity matching first ranks rooms, then views, then objects. Only when fast scores are ambiguous does a vision-language model inspect the narrowed multimodal evidence and refine the selection. The semantic module outputs a goal object/view or goal pose; conventional localization, planning, and humanoid control execute it. It is not an RGB-to-joint VLA policy.

This conditional compute is the main engineering contribution. Long tour videos waste VLM context on irrelevant frames and make response time grow with route length. Structured coarse-to-fine retrieval bounds context, preserves the image evidence needed to distinguish instances, and reserves expensive reasoning for difficult cases. Across four humanoid-collected indoor datasets and 87 varied instructions, the paper reports leading retrieval success and an 82% response-time reduction relative to VLM reasoning over tour video, followed by integration with speech and Unitree G1 navigation.

The evidence mainly validates goal retrieval, not every downstream navigation failure. A prebuilt graph can become stale, and room/view segmentation or embedding mistakes may discard the correct candidate before slow reasoning ever sees it. Thresholds governing fast-versus-slow escalation trade latency against missed goals, yet confidence from modern multimodal models is often poorly calibrated. Online graph maintenance, retrieval recall guarantees, uncertainty-aware escalation, movable-object memory, and end-to-end SPL/collision evaluations would better establish how semantic speedups translate to physical autonomy.

### Fast/slow timing and execution boundary

The fast branch acts on the current view and compact graph context, handling obvious waypoint following, turning, and instruction progress without repeatedly invoking a large reasoner. The slow branch is triggered by ambiguity, long-horizon choice, or failure and queries the hierarchical scene graph for rooms, objects, viewpoints, and relationships. It returns a subgoal or revised route, not motor torque. A geometric controller still verifies free space and executes that subgoal.

This division saves inference only if triggers are calibrated. Too many slow calls erase latency benefits; too few let the fast policy persist with bad grounding. Evaluation should publish calls and latency per episode, trigger precision, graph size, replanning frequency, and recovery after corrupted detections. A probabilistic timestamped graph would prevent stale relations from becoming facts, while a value-of-information test could invoke slow reasoning only when expected improvement exceeds cost. The cleanest comparison would hold perception and low-level control fixed and vary only memory hierarchy and trigger policy.

### Scene-graph schema, reasoning schedule, and benchmark interpretation

The hierarchical graph must preserve metric facts needed for execution as well as semantic facts needed for instruction grounding. Viewpoint nodes connect observations to pose; object nodes carry category, attributes, image evidence, and 3-D location; region or room nodes summarize navigable context; edges encode containment, adjacency, relative direction, and visibility. The fast module can consult a compressed neighborhood around the current node, whereas slow reasoning traverses coarse-to-fine structure and revises a subgoal when the instruction requires unseen context.

Slow-reasoning triggers are a learned or engineered policy in their own right. Ambiguous noun grounding, conflicting observations, loss of progress, or arrival at a decision junction are sensible triggers; fixed periodic calls waste compute. The graph should record which evidence led to a decision so re-perception can target uncertainty rather than merely repeat a view. Results across MP3D, HM3D, and RoboTHOR suggest useful zero-shot transfer, but simulator differences in action granularity, depth, object labels, and navigation mesh complicate aggregate comparisons.

The strongest ablations would equalize perception and LLM model, then remove hierarchy, fast/slow scheduling, re-perception, or individual graph modalities. Report success, SPL, physical distance, collisions, LLM calls/tokens, latency, and recovery after a deliberately wrong node. I would also test instruction compositionality—relations, ordering, and negative constraints—because simple ObjectNav can be solved mostly with room priors. The method becomes most compelling when structured memory resolves language that current-view prompting cannot and does so with fewer expensive reasoning calls.

Repeated runs with identical observations should additionally quantify language-model decision variance and whether that variance materially changes path safety, efficiency, or recovery behavior.

## GuideWalk: Learning Unified Autonomous Navigation and Locomotion for Humanoid Robots across Versatile Terrains

GuideWalk jointly learns navigation guidance and dynamically stable humanoid locomotion across slopes, stairs, beams, and clutter. A traversability-aware guidance module turns terrain observations and goals into feasible local commands, while a low-level whole-body policy executes them. Training uses simulation, terrain curricula, and privileged information for robust deployment. The novelty is closing the usual gap between a geometric planner that assumes ideal tracking and a locomotion policy with limited feasible actions. Results show one policy coordinating avoidance and traversal across varied terrain. Long-horizon semantic navigation and formal safety guarantees remain outside its main scope.

### Teacher composition, student observations, and policy learning

GuideWalk first constructs a privileged expert by composing two capabilities: a Dynamic Window Approach (DWA) navigation teacher uses ground-truth geometry to choose local planar velocity/yaw guidance, and a locomotion teacher converts that guidance into dynamically feasible full-body motion. The deployable student does not merely copy the DWA command. It maps partial onboard observations directly to joint-level actions, so navigation choices and body responses can co-adapt. Training combines reinforcement learning with DAgger-style action imitation. Imitation anchors the student to competent teacher rollouts, while the RL objective permits actions that the separated teachers do not express—for example lateral correction, body-height adjustment, or arm retraction when a nominal planar command would collide.

The observation is multimodal and embodiment-aware: an 18 x 32 forward depth image, a local elevation map, target position, obstacle field-of-view information, proprioceptive state, and the previous action. These signals capture both what lies ahead and whether the humanoid is currently capable of following a route. Privileged quantities are available to teachers/critics in simulation but not required by the deployed actor. The output is a vector of whole-body joint targets consumed by the robot's low-level servo layer, rather than a waypoint for an independent gait controller. This explains why the method can coordinate leg placement and upper-body clearance while still pursuing the navigation goal.

The strongest idea is the hybrid objective. Pure DWA imitation would preserve the teacher's simplifying assumptions, whereas unconstrained RL would face a very sparse, unstable search over navigation and humanoid balance. GuideWalk uses the teacher as a behavioral prior, then allows task reward to repair the mismatch between planar planning and actual whole-body feasibility. The result should be read as learned local goal navigation across versatile geometry, not semantic long-horizon autonomy.

There are also revealing engineering costs. Supplying both a depth image and an elevation map gives complementary representations but may require redundant sensing and mapping hardware; the authors explicitly identify reconstructing the map from depth as future work. A small depth raster and local horizon can hide thin or distant hazards, and teacher-induced exploration may leave rare recovery states underrepresented. Further work should test recurrent memory for occlusion, calibrated uncertainty, moving obstacles, explicit fall/recovery behavior, and ablations separating gains from RL, DAgger, privileged teaching, and each exteroceptive channel.

### Teacher–student information flow and causal deployment

GuideWalk trains two cooperating teachers. Navigation guidance converts target position and privileged obstacle knowledge into desired planar linear and angular velocity. A locomotion teacher takes those commands plus privileged terrain/state and outputs full-body joint actions using PPO. The deployable student receives only onboard-available robot state, target information, and obstacles inside a depth-camera-like field of view; behavior cloning from the teachers is combined with RL so it learns useful actions while remaining stable in states caused by its own perception errors.

The contribution is unifying route choice and gait adaptation in one causal student instead of giving a blind locomotion policy a waypoint it cannot safely realize. The student can slow, turn, step, or reorient its body according to terrain, while privileged critics/teachers make exploration tractable. But joint training can obscure which layer failed. Results should separate goal error, collision, foot slip, fall, commanded-versus-real velocity, and performance under depth dropout or latency. A valuable next step is a short predictive safety layer with map uncertainty, plus explicit recovery or reverse actions when the local observation shows that the chosen approach is no longer feasible.

### Optimization objective, observation asymmetry, and the main scientific claim

The locomotion teacher is trained with asymmetric information: the actor or critic can use exact terrain, obstacle, root, contact, or dynamics state available in simulation, while the eventual student is restricted to a camera-like field of view and onboard proprioception. Navigation guidance supplies desired linear and angular velocity based on target and obstacles. PPO provides task return and state-distribution exploration; an auxiliary behavior-cloning loss transfers the teacher's coordinated joint actions. Keeping RL in student training matters because pure BC is brittle after a small perception error moves the humanoid off the teacher trajectory.

Unified learning should be tested against two modular baselines: global/local navigation feeding a strong blind locomotion policy, and perceptive locomotion receiving an externally planned velocity. If GuideWalk improves only goal success, it may be choosing easier routes; if it improves slip/fall at equal routes, the benefit is locomotion coupling. Metrics should include path efficiency, arrival orientation, terrain class, fall/collision, foot clearance, velocity tracking, action smoothness, and compute latency. Oracle depth and privileged-observation evaluations quantify the remaining perception gap.

The paper's main scientific claim is that target-directed navigation and full-body terrain negotiation benefit from one policy-level representation. That is plausible on rough terrain where the “best” velocity depends on the next viable foot placements. The danger is loss of interpretability and global foresight: a local student can learn conservative shortcuts or enter cul-de-sacs. A strong extension would retain a topological/global route, add a learned short-horizon feasibility and uncertainty head, and expose these to a safety MPC. This preserves end-to-end coupling locally without demanding that one network remember an entire environment.

## Gallant Voxel Grid-based Humanoid Locomotion and Local-navigation across 3D Constrained Terrains

Gallant uses a local 3-D voxel representation to let a humanoid negotiate constrained terrain where a height map is insufficient, including obstacles affecting the torso and limbs. The learned policy consumes proprioception, goal information, and voxelized surroundings and outputs whole-body actions or joint targets. Training randomizes terrain and obstacles in simulation. Its central contribution is unifying local navigation, collision avoidance, and stable locomotion in a compact volumetric policy rather than planning for the base and body separately. Voxel resolution and sensing range bound the detail and horizon, so a global planner is still useful for large environments.

### Voxel encoder, observation history, and control rates

Gallant voxelizes LiDAR into a torso-centered 32 x 32 x 40 binary grid at 5-cm resolution. This preserves floors, ceilings, lateral clutter, platforms, and drops without the cost of a general 3-D network. The actor additionally sees a relative target/time command and six-frame histories of base angular velocity, projected gravity, joint positions/velocities, and previous actions. Its critic receives privileged base linear velocity and a height map. Non-voxel signals pass through a two-layer, 256-D Mish/LayerNorm MLP; a three-layer 2-D CNN treats the 40 vertical bins as channels and emits a 64-D geometric feature. Fusion produces a 256-D latent, followed by 29 joint targets for the G1 actor and a scalar value for the critic.

Treating height as channels is the architectural highlight: 2-D kernels efficiently model horizontal layout while channel mixing captures vertical obstruction. PPO training spans 1,024 x 8 environments over eight curricula. The raycaster includes the robot's moving links, self-scans, and occlusion holes, plus randomized LiDAR mounting, hit noise, 100–200 ms latency, and 2% missing voxels. On hardware, two Hesai JT128 sensors approximate spherical coverage; voxel updates run at 10 Hz, policy at 50 Hz, and PD at 500 Hz. A separate head Mid-360 with Fast-LIO2 supplies the local target at 25 Hz.

The result is one local policy that crouches, clears doors, climbs stairs/platforms, and crosses clutter where height maps fail. Ablations favor the height-channel CNN over heavier 3-D/sparse alternatives and show that simulated self-occlusion/latency matters for transfer. Still, binary cells omit confidence, surface normal, age, support semantics, and velocity; 5 cm is coarse for footholds, while finer grids reduce field of view. Ten-Hz scans are weak around dynamic obstacles. Multiresolution, probabilistic, time-aware voxels plus a global planner and independent safety fallback are the clearest extensions.

### Why voxels matter and what the controller must infer

Gallant retains occupied space in three dimensions rather than collapsing it to a height map. A robot-centered voxel grid can represent ceilings, rails, table edges, narrow gaps, and simultaneous floor/overhang surfaces. The learned controller combines this local geometry with proprioception and a navigation command and outputs whole-body targets, allowing torso and arms to participate in clearance while legs preserve support. Training with randomized constrained terrain teaches the network to associate geometric patterns with crouching, turning, stepping, and body reconfiguration.

The representation is more expressive but more expensive and sparse than a 2.5-D grid. Resolution sets a hard trade-off: coarse voxels erase thin obstacles; fine voxels increase memory, latency, and the data required for convolutional features. Localization error can shift the entire obstacle volume relative to the body. Evaluation should report voxel size/range, map age, minimum link clearance, and success under pose perturbations, not just arrival rate. Sparse hierarchical voxels, uncertainty channels, swept-volume checking, and temporal fusion of moving obstacles would strengthen the system. A global planner remains necessary because a local voxel policy can still enter a geometrically valid dead end.

### Voxel encoder design, temporal memory, and appropriate baselines

A robot-centered voxel tensor must encode occupancy and often distance, height, or semantic channels over a finite 3-D extent. Sparse or dense 3-D convolutions turn it into geometry features that are fused with target-relative position, base orientation and velocity, joint state, and previous action. The actor outputs whole-body desired joints or residual targets; an asymmetric critic may use privileged scene and contact state. A temporal encoder or recurrent state is important because a single voxel snapshot cannot distinguish a newly revealed gap from a mapping dropout or estimate obstacle motion.

The correct representation ablation compares identical policies using height maps, multilayer elevation, raw depth, and voxels at matched physical range and compute. Performance should be separated into low overhang, narrow lateral gap, floor obstacle, vertical step, and compound constraints. Voxel resolution and update frequency must be reported because they determine whether clearance claims are meaningful. Oracle voxels versus reconstructed onboard voxels quantify perception error, while pose-noise sweeps show how quickly whole-body collision margin disappears.

Gallant's highlight is allowing geometry around shoulders, hands, torso, knees, and feet to influence one action. Yet generic occupancy gives no distinction between collision, support, and graspable/bracing surfaces. It also provides limited global escape logic. Distance-transform and uncertainty channels would make gradients more useful; semantic/contact-affordance channels could permit safe support; and a continuous collision checker could project the network's target. Hierarchical sparse grids should preserve fine cells near the body and coarser look-ahead, lowering cost without erasing the details that matter for a squeeze.

## HA-VLN 2.0: An Open Benchmark and Leaderboard for Human-Aware Navigation in Discrete and Continuous Environments with Dynamic Multi-Human Interactions

[HA-VLN](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.14229) is primarily a benchmark rather than a controller. It unifies discrete panoramic and continuous-control vision-language navigation while adding dynamic people, personal-space constraints, and socially meaningful language. HAPS 2.0, upgraded simulators, 16,844 human-centric instructions, real-robot validation, and a public leaderboard expose failures hidden by static ObjectNav/VLN tests. The main lesson is that task success alone is insufficient: collision rate and social compliance must also be measured. Benchmark results improve when social context is modeled, but simulation realism and culturally variable notions of appropriate distance remain open issues.

### Task definition, data, and what the benchmark actually tests

HA-VLN 2.0 deliberately spans two action regimes: graph-constrained panoramic choices used by classic VLN and continuous free-space motion closer to a deployed robot. Episodes pair language with environments containing several moving humans, partial observations, personal-space constraints, outdoor/indoor contexts, and motion-language references. HAPS 2.0 expands human trajectories and refines alignment between what people are doing and how instructions describe them. In total, 16,844 human-centric instructions support standardized evaluation, and released simulators, agent code, leaderboard, and real-robot trials make comparisons reproducible.

Because this is a benchmark, there is no single mandated network input/output. Baselines receive visual observations, instruction tokens, pose/history, and—depending on the track—candidate viewpoints or continuous observations; they output the next discrete viewpoint/action or continuous navigation command. Metrics must jointly reflect goal success/path efficiency, collisions, and infringement of human comfort rather than rewarding the shortest geometric route alone. Experiments show that explicitly modeling social context reduces collision and improves navigation relative to agents trained for static VLN.

The contribution is therefore diagnostic: it exposes how an agent that scores well in empty scans can fail when several partially observed people move and language implicitly constrains passing behavior. Real-world crowded-indoor tests add credibility, but simulated motion diversity, visual realism, and fixed personal-space rules cannot capture culture, intent, group formation, or vulnerable users. A next benchmark version should separate prediction, etiquette, and emergency avoidance errors; report near-misses and minimum time-to-collision; include interactive humans who respond to the robot; and evaluate calibrated uncertainty and recovery, not only final success.

### What the benchmark measures—and does not

HA-VLN 2.0 varies whether navigation occurs on a discrete connectivity graph or in continuous control, and introduces multiple moving humans whose paths and interactions change what constitutes a good trajectory. An agent must satisfy the language instruction while avoiding collision, respecting personal space, and adapting when people block an intended path. The benchmark therefore needs task success and path efficiency together with human-aware quantities such as collision count, closest approach, discomfort-zone intrusion, waiting or detour behavior, and performance versus crowd density.

Dynamic humans expose a key distinction between prediction and etiquette. Extrapolating motion may avoid impact but still cut through a group or pass uncomfortably close; a socially conservative agent may freeze and never finish. Models should receive clear observations—RGB/depth or simulator state depending on track—and disclose whether future human trajectories are privileged. Strong evaluation separates perception, forecasting, language grounding, and control. Useful future tracks would include humans reacting to the robot, culturally/task-dependent personal space, uncertain intent, wheel versus leg embodiments, and real-robot validation. Leaderboard scores should not compress safety and efficiency into one number without publishing the Pareto trade-off.

### Dataset protocol, metrics, and social-policy caveats

Discrete and continuous tracks test different capabilities. A graph action can abstract away acceleration, footprint, and low-level collision, while continuous motion exposes control delay and how an agent passes a person. Results should never be compared without stating the track and observation regime. Dynamic multi-human episodes also require fixed or seeded human policies so two agents encounter comparable difficulty. If humans replay open-loop paths, the benchmark measures avoidance around movers; if they react, it measures interaction and can produce policy-dependent scenarios.

Human-aware metrics should be reported individually. Navigation success and SPL reward efficiency; collision and minimum separation capture physical safety; personal-space intrusion approximates discomfort; time spent stopped reveals freezing; path deviation and acceleration reflect disruption to the robot's task. A single weighted score embeds contestable social values. Pareto curves over safety and efficiency are more informative, along with breakdowns by density, crossing angle, group formation, and instruction complexity.

Benchmark leakage is another concern: language and room layouts may repeat, simulated pedestrians may have recognizable motion, and privileged human positions can make perception unrealistically easy. Strong submissions should declare detector, tracker, prediction horizon, map access, and whether human intent is observed. Future versions should include sensor noise, partial occlusion, children/wheelchairs/carts as distinct geometries, people who yield or communicate, and real-robot episodes with consent and conservative speeds. The benchmark's value lies in forcing these assumptions into the open, not in crowning one opaque composite score.

## Hier-SLAM: Scaling-up Semantics in SLAM with a Hierarchically Categorical Gaussian Splatting

Hier-SLAM adds scalable semantics to dense Gaussian-splatting SLAM. Rather than attach one flat, fixed class label to every primitive, it organizes categories hierarchically so a map can represent coarse structures, object groups, and fine classes without an exploding semantic state. Camera tracking and Gaussian-map optimization share the geometric/appearance representation, while semantic observations update categorical features. The result supports photorealistic reconstruction and queryable semantic maps for planning. The hierarchy is the key novelty and improves scalability, though the system inherits dependencies on visual tracking, segmentation quality, and compute-heavy dense mapping.

### Hierarchical encoding and joint SLAM optimization

Hier-SLAM accepts an RGB-D stream and incrementally maintains camera poses plus a global 3-D Gaussian map. Each Gaussian stores center, radius/covariance, opacity, color, and a semantic embedding. Incoming poses are initialized with a constant-velocity model and refined against the fixed map using rendered color and depth losses over pixels with sufficient silhouette visibility. Mapping then fixes the camera poses and optimizes geometry, appearance, and semantic parameters jointly. Consequently its primary outputs are a metrically tracked trajectory and renderable color/depth/semantic maps—not navigation actions.

The semantic novelty is a class tree rather than one independent vector dimension per leaf class. Before SLAM, GPT-4o-mini groups the supplied class vocabulary from leaves toward progressively coarser parents; a validator/critic loop checks omitted labels, and tree construction stops when the coarse level is sufficiently compact. This LLM is an offline taxonomy builder, not an online navigator. Every Gaussian receives embeddings along its root-to-leaf path. An inter-level loss learns predictions at individual resolutions, while a cross-level loss flattens the combined hierarchy back to the leaf target, keeping coarse and fine meanings mutually consistent. Training schedules the cross-level term after initial inter-level fitting.

This representation is most convincing as a memory-scaling contribution: the reported system handles over 500 semantic classes and can render coarse-to-fine interpretations while reducing semantic storage and optimization time. It also avoids carrying a large vision-language feature at every primitive. The tradeoff is that open-world understanding is only as good as the predefined labels and offline tree. Ambiguous objects may reasonably have multiple parents, whereas the chosen taxonomy imposes one path; an LLM-generated grouping can also encode arbitrary ontology errors. RGB-D blur and noisy depth still degrade tracking, and segmentation mistakes can be baked into the map. Useful extensions are online insertion/reparenting of categories, uncertainty over hierarchical labels, loop closure and long-term map maintenance, and task-conditioned rendering that measures whether the hierarchy actually improves robot decisions rather than only 2-D mIoU.

### Hierarchical semantic code and map outputs

Hier-SLAM attaches categorical information to 3-D Gaussians while avoiding one feature channel for every open-set label. An LLM organizes input categories into a hierarchy; compact codes at coarse and fine levels are learned with inter-level and cross-level consistency losses. RGB-D frames update camera pose and Gaussian geometry/appearance, while segmented labels supervise semantic attributes. Rendering then produces color, depth, or semantic maps, and object/category queries can be answered at different abstraction levels.

The hierarchy makes memory scale more gracefully and lets related labels share structure, but its taxonomy is a prior that can be wrong. A category omitted or placed under the wrong parent may be confused systematically, and changing an open vocabulary can require code reassignment. Evaluation should include new-category insertion, long sequences, hierarchy depth, memory per Gaussian, semantic boundary quality, and pose error—not only mean segmentation accuracy. Probabilistic codes and editable local taxonomy updates would help. For navigation, uncertainty should propagate into object search so a coarse “furniture” belief can guide exploration without being treated as a verified “chair.”

### Losses, code capacity, and navigation-facing queries

In addition to photometric and depth reconstruction, Hier-SLAM applies semantic objectives that keep codes discriminative within a hierarchy and consistent across levels. Coarse classes can share compact dimensions while fine leaves retain identity; a validator checks or repairs the LLM-generated taxonomy before training. Each Gaussian stores geometry, opacity, appearance, and hierarchical categorical parameters. Differentiable splatting renders predicted RGB, depth, and category distributions, jointly refining map and camera pose from RGB-D sequences.

Capacity allocation is the central trade-off. A flat one-hot map grows linearly with categories, while a very compact hierarchy forces unrelated labels to collide. The paper should report semantic memory per Gaussian, rendering and update FPS, hierarchy construction cost, number of classes/leaves, and performance when categories are added after mapping begins. Boundary IoU and instance consistency matter because average pixel accuracy can be dominated by walls and floors. Pose ablations reveal whether better semantics actually improve SLAM or only inherit its geometry.

For navigation, the useful API is not merely a rendered label image. Queries should return likely object instances, 3-D extent, confidence, hierarchy ancestors, best observation viewpoints, and reachability context. A search for an unseen “office chair” can first use coarse furniture evidence and refine after approaching. Because taxonomy errors are global, the system needs reversible local relabeling and synonym handling rather than rebuilding the map. Dynamic objects should leave a separate time-indexed track. My judgment is that hierarchical coding is a genuine scaling tool, but its downstream benefit must be demonstrated through object search or instruction grounding, not semantic rendering alone.

## HoloAgent-0: A Unified Embodied Agent Framework with 3D Spatial Memory

[HoloAgent-0](https://arxiv.org/abs/2606.23565) is a robot-agent framework rather than a single navigation network. An Embodied AgentOS converts language requests into skill graphs, schedules resources, monitors execution, and replans or asks for clarification. Persistent 3-D spatial memory grounds object and place references, while embodiment-specific skills execute navigation, motion, and manipulation. The achievement is a common closed-loop interface across heterogeneous robots and long-horizon tasks. Its strength is modularity and runtime feedback; system performance is consequently bounded by the reliability of every skill, memory update, and high-level language decision, and broad evaluations do not replace component-level safety guarantees.

### AgentOS loop, grounded memory, and skill boundary

The three coupled layers have deliberately different outputs. Embodied AgentOS parses a language request into a directed skill graph, resolves dependencies/resources, starts skills, watches typed runtime feedback, and can replan or ask for clarification after failure. The 3-D spatial-memory layer maintains persistent metric/semantic entities and relations so phrases such as “the object beside the table” refer to world state rather than a transient image. The skill layer wraps embodiment-specific navigation, motion generation, search, and manipulation controllers behind common preconditions, arguments, status, and results.

Accordingly, no single history length, Transformer, or action vector describes HoloAgent-0. Foundation/language models operate at planning and grounding level; the invoked skills may use geometric planning, learned perception, RL locomotion, or robot-specific control. Inputs include the user instruction, memory query results, skill feedback, and live perception. Outputs are skill-graph revisions and API-level calls, eventually translated by each skill into robot motion. Evaluations cover spatial memory, long-horizon search/navigation, cross-robot cooperation, motion generation, and mobile manipulation on hardware.

The highlight is bringing the familiar tool-use agent loop—plan, execute, inspect, revise—into a physical system while keeping continuous safety-critical control outside the LLM. Persistent 3-D grounding also reduces repeated rediscovery and makes cross-robot handoff possible. But a shared interface does not make heterogeneous skills equally reliable, and an incorrect memory identity can poison several later decisions. High-level replanning is too slow for collision safety and must remain subordinate to certified/local controllers. Future work should formalize skill contracts and uncertainty, maintain provenance and temporal validity in memory, verify plans before execution, bound resource conflicts, and publish failure taxonomies showing whether errors arise in language planning, grounding, memory, or physical skills.

### Memory/action loop and model boundary

HoloAgent-0's useful abstraction is a persistent 3-D memory that stores observations in a common spatial frame and exposes them to planning, rather than asking a multimodal model to reconstruct layout from a short image history. New RGB-D or reconstructed geometry updates objects, free space, viewpoints, and semantic attributes; a high-level agent queries this memory with the instruction and chooses a goal or interaction. A conventional navigation stack converts that decision into collision-checked motion. The foundation model reasons over a compact world state, not raw point clouds at servo rate.

This separation supports long-horizon tasks and revisiting, but errors become persistent: pose drift duplicates objects, association merges instances, and stale movable objects remain in memory. The system should attach timestamps, provenance, confidence, and negative observations to every entity and explicitly reconcile contradictions. Evaluation needs memory growth, query latency, loop/revisit consistency, and task degradation under injected pose or detection errors. Active perception should select views that reduce uncertainty, while low-level safety must remain independent of language-model output. The central question is not whether the model can describe a remembered room, but whether memory produces better, cheaper, and more recoverable actions than image-history prompting.

### Spatial-memory schema, planning cadence, and grounding tests

Persistent memory should contain at least camera/keyframe poses, observed free and occupied geometry, object hypotheses with multiple views, navigable connectivity, and task-relevant relations. Retrieval must combine semantic similarity with spatial filters and recency; otherwise a large model sees an unordered database rather than a world. HoloAgent-0's unified framework is most useful when the same memory supports object search, route planning, revisiting, and manipulation-oriented final views. High-level reasoning runs when a subgoal changes, while local mapping and control update much faster.

Long-horizon evaluation should measure more than final success. Memory association accuracy, duplicated/forgotten objects, loop consistency, retrieval latency, storage growth, number of foundation-model calls, and recovery after an object moves expose whether 3-D memory actually works. “No-memory,” image-history, 2-D map, and oracle-memory baselines clarify whether gains come from spatial structure or simply longer context. Tasks requiring returning to an earlier object or reasoning about rooms provide stronger evidence than short direct navigation.

The difficult failure is mutually reinforcing geometry and semantics. Drift can place two copies of an object; the semantic model may interpret them as distinct and plan an unnecessary search. Conversely, confident category similarity can cause an incorrect geometric merge. Factorized uncertainty and provenance let the agent revisit the exact view that would disambiguate the hypotheses. The low-level planner must reject unreachable language-model goals and report why. A mature version would treat memory operations—insert, merge, split, invalidate, verify—as explicit actions with costs, making the system an active mapping agent rather than a passive prompt cache.

## Holistic Fusion: Task- and Setup-Agnostic Robot Localization and State Estimation with Factor Graphs

Holistic Fusion builds a configurable factor-graph estimator that can combine IMU, joint kinematics, contact, cameras, LiDAR, GNSS, or other measurements without redesigning an estimator for every robot and task. Each source contributes factors to a shared state trajectory, and robust optimization resolves their complementary constraints. The novelty is a task/setup-agnostic software and modeling framework rather than a new navigation policy. It improves resilience to individual sensor failures and supplies globally consistent state to downstream mapping and control. Accuracy still depends on valid noise models, calibration, synchronization, and correct contact assumptions.

### Joint local/global state and dynamic context variables

Holistic Fusion formulates both robot state and reference-frame relationships as variables in one factor graph. It accepts an arbitrary number of absolute measurements (for example GNSS/global pose), local relative-motion estimates, and landmark observations expressed in different frames. Instead of demanding pre-alignment, it estimates frame transforms and other “context” variables jointly and lets them evolve as random walks when they may drift. This covers sensor extrinsics, map/world alignment, landmarks, or other setup-specific quantities without changing the conceptual estimator.

The implementation gives special attention to the tension between global correction and control-quality local motion. Global observations suppress long-term drift, but an abrupt graph correction can destabilize a controller. HF produces a low-latency, smooth local belief while maintaining globally accurate localization, propagating results at IMU rate on ordinary robot computers. Five real scenarios on three platforms demonstrate different modality/configuration combinations; there is no neural network or RL policy at the core, and output is a state distribution/trajectory for downstream mapping and control.

The strongest contribution is usability and a common abstraction: adding a measurement type means defining its factor rather than rebuilding a bespoke fusion pipeline. Modeling context in the graph also makes automatic frame alignment part of estimation rather than fragile preprocessing. Generality does not remove observability, however. Unconstrained frame transforms, correlated front-end errors treated as independent, timestamp offsets, contact-model failures, and incorrectly chosen random-walk noise can still yield consistent-looking but wrong estimates. Online observability checks, automated noise learning, robust switchable factors, and explicit compute/memory behavior over lifelong missions are important extensions.

### Factors, state variables, and why “task agnostic” matters

Holistic Fusion represents poses, velocities, IMU biases, sensor extrinsics, and possibly contact or kinematic states as variables in a factor graph. IMU preintegration, LiDAR or visual odometry, leg kinematics, contacts, and other setup-specific measurements contribute factors with uncertainty; optimization finds the trajectory most consistent with all of them. Adding a sensor means adding a calibrated factor rather than redesigning one monolithic filter. The output is a state estimate and covariance consumed by mapping and control, not a navigation command.

Task agnosticism is credible only when observability and synchronization are handled explicitly. Foot contact may constrain velocity on firm ground but fail during slip; cameras fail in darkness; LiDAR degenerates in corridors; inaccurate extrinsics create mutually inconsistent factors. Robust kernels can suppress an outlier but cannot repair a biased sensor indefinitely. Evaluation should include sensor-removal tests, time-offset and extrinsic perturbations, slip, compute latency, and covariance consistency. Online calibration, switchable factors, and directional degeneracy information would let the graph degrade gracefully. A planner should receive uncertainty so it can slow or seek informative motion instead of assuming every fused pose is equally trustworthy.

### Factor construction, observability, and estimator health

IMU preintegration summarizes many high-rate accelerometer and gyroscope readings between graph states while retaining bias Jacobians. Visual or LiDAR factors constrain relative pose against images or geometry; leg kinematic factors use joint encoders and contact assumptions; zero-velocity/contact factors reduce drift when a foot is trusted. Optional priors such as GNSS, wheel odometry, or known landmarks can be added without changing the state definition. Fixed-lag smoothing bounds computation, whereas a global graph can retain loop closures at higher cost.

The “holistic” claim should be evaluated through modularity and consistency rather than maximum sensor count. For each sensor combination, report trajectory error, state latency, compute, covariance calibration, and graceful degradation after dropout. Contact factors must be disabled or down-weighted during slip; robust losses and switchable constraints prevent one false contact or visual match from corrupting the solution. Online temporal and extrinsic calibration are especially important on articulated robots whose sensors vibrate and heat.

No fusion method can observe every direction in every environment. The graph should expose rank/conditioning and marginal covariance, allowing a controller to slow when heading or height is uncertain and an active system to seek informative motion. Residual monitoring should distinguish a failed sensor from an inaccurate process model. My judgment is that a task-agnostic factor library is valuable infrastructure, but reproducibility requires exact factor definitions, noise models, timestamp handling, window sizes, and solver rates. Otherwise “fusion” improvements may come from dataset-specific tuning that does not transfer to a new robot setup.

## High-speed control and navigation for quadrupedal robots on complex and discrete terrain (2025.06)(Science Robotics 2025)

This work targets fast, autonomous quadruped travel across complex and discontinuous terrain. A perception and planning layer reasons about local geometry and feasible foothold corridors, while a learned or optimized locomotion controller executes aggressive motion and rejects disturbances. Tight coupling is essential: the navigator selects routes the body can actually traverse, and the controller exposes agility beyond conservative flat-ground planning. The reported real-world experiments emphasize speed on discrete terrain. Remaining risks are depth/map error at velocity, limited look-ahead, and sim-to-real behavior on obstacle geometries not covered by training.

### Foothold search and 100-Hz RL tracking

The stack explicitly separates contact choice from dynamic execution. A rapidly exploring random-tree-style planner searches feasible future footholds over a 2.5-D terrain map; a boundary-estimation network identifies usable support regions, and costs enforce reachability, collision, and route preference. An RL tracker then receives upcoming front/rear foothold targets and produces joint commands. Planning is faster than target consumption, leaving margin for replanning rather than committing to one offline sequence.

The PPO actor uses a 167-dimensional observation combining current proprioception, samples from 10, 20, and 30 ms of proprioceptive history, future contact targets, and an estimated base linear velocity. A GRU estimator ingests current proprioception and the action from 10 ms earlier; its 128-D state feeds a [64,16] MLP velocity head. Actor and critic are [512,128] MLPs, the actor emits 12 joint-position targets at 100 Hz, and a separate four-output estimator predicts foot contacts. Rewards distinguish accurate touchdown from merely passing near a target and regularize physical feasibility and motion style.

Training terrain is adversarially curriculum-generated. A CVAE parameterizes stepping-stone size, spacing, orientation, and yaw; difficulty expands with tracking competence, producing informative near-limit failures rather than mostly trivial or impossible scenes. Hardware tests include stairs, gaps, isolated pads, parkour sequences, and an approximately 0.6-m ascent/descent.

The visible foothold plan is easier to inspect and retune than an end-to-end image policy, while the learned tracker handles impacts and model error better than a purely analytic controller. Dependence on a 2.5-D map, binary support assumptions, and state accuracy remains substantial; overhangs, deformable/moving surfaces, and late map changes are not naturally handled. Future planning should optimize calibrated touchdown probability and impact/thermal cost, use richer geometry where required, and include a reactive abort/recovery policy when committed footholds become invalid.

### High-speed perception–planning–control contract

At high speed, sensing must describe not only current footholds but terrain far enough ahead to cover braking and gait transition distance. The navigation policy or planner consumes a local terrain representation, robot velocity/state, and a route target, then produces a short-horizon path or velocity/foothold command. A faster locomotion controller converts it into joint behavior. Training or optimization must expose discrete gaps, stepping stones, edges, and landing regions where a small state-estimation delay changes feasibility abruptly.

The scientific value is demonstrated only when speed, autonomy, and terrain difficulty occur together. A manually aligned run or known obstacle sequence tests control but not navigation. Results should disclose perception range and rate, controller rate, maximum map age, commanded/real speed, intervention count, landing error, impact, and failure versus gap/stone size. Predictive uncertainty should enlarge margins with speed, and an emergency braking or safe-stop behavior must be feasible on the remaining support. Future work can jointly optimize viewpoint and body motion, because pitching for a jump may temporarily hide the next landing from the camera.

### Look-ahead geometry, latency, and near-limit evaluation

At velocity `v`, perception and control must reserve at least `v` times total sensing-to-actuation delay plus the physical stopping or gait-transition distance. Discrete terrain makes this constraint unforgiving: an error of one foothold can turn a valid gap crossing into a fall. The system therefore needs far-field geometry for route and speed selection, fine near-field geometry for contacts, and state estimation accurate during pitch, impact, and flight. A terrain encoder may propose a path or foothold sequence, while a high-rate policy tracks it and compensates dynamics.

Training should randomize not only terrain dimensions but perception corruption, map delay, friction, compliance, payload, actuator strength, and landing state. A curriculum that expands speed and obstacle difficulty together can otherwise overfit to a fixed approach. Oracle-terrain and onboard-perception comparisons isolate sensing; oracle-state tests isolate estimation; commanded-path tracking isolates control. Repeated hardware trials near the reported limit are essential because a single successful video is weak statistical evidence.

Useful metrics include success confidence intervals, mean and worst landing error, minimum support margin, impact force, peak power/torque, slip, stop distance, map age, and intervention. A controller that is fast only because it accepts high hardware stress is not generally superior. The next step should attach calibrated feasibility to candidate footholds and maintain an abort trajectory before commitment. During flight, when no braking is possible, uncertainty must be resolved before takeoff. That asymmetry makes predictive sensing and conservative commitment more important than reactive recovery alone.

## Learning Autonomous and Safe Quadruped Traversal of Complex Terrains Using Multi-Layer Elevation Maps

The paper represents terrain with multiple elevation layers so bridges, steps, overhangs, and stacked surfaces are not collapsed into a single height. From these maps it estimates traversability and plans routes, while a learned locomotion policy follows commands over rough ground. The main novelty is bringing a richer yet planning-friendly terrain model into an autonomous legged stack. This improves safety over ordinary 2.5-D maps in vertically complex scenes. It still discretizes geometry and depends on accurate localization and map fusion; unseen dynamic obstacles require an additional reactive layer.

### Multi-layer representation and hierarchical policies

Each horizontal cell holds several non-intersecting vertical surface intervals, representing floor and tabletop, bridge underside and deck, or several stacked obstacles without paying for a full voxel volume. Convolutional encoders process regular layers, while a Transformer handles variable-length surface elements. A terrain compressor is trained with L1 reconstruction loss (roughly 3.0-cm train and 4.9-cm test MAE), and its features condition a local navigation policy.

Below navigation, several terrain-specialist locomotion policies are trained and distilled into one generalist. The deployed locomotion actor observes desired body velocity, joint positions/velocities, proprioception, its previous 12-D action, and the multi-layer map, and emits 12 joint-position targets. During distillation the student sees extra clutter not shown to every expert, increasing robustness without destabilizing specialist training. Navigation fuses goal and map features to command this generalist. PPO rewards goal progress, collision avoidance, maneuverability, and stable execution, with terrain curricula and symmetry augmentation.

The representation is the durable contribution: it retains grid/CNN efficiency while expressing multiple surfaces at identical `(x,y)`, enabling routes beneath overhangs and across constrained platforms that one-height maps cannot describe. But “safe” here is empirical reward shaping and randomized evaluation, not a verified shield. Mean height error is also a weak metric at a clearance boundary: a small average error can create false free space exactly where a head or flank collides. Fixed layer count/resolution loses thin structure and has no natural dynamic-obstacle state. Per-layer confidence and timestamps, swept-body collision filtering, false-free-space metrics, and equal-memory comparisons against sparse voxels/SDFs would strengthen the safety and representation claims.

### Map layers, policy interface, and safety interpretation

A multi-layer elevation map preserves several surfaces at one horizontal location, allowing the policy to distinguish ground from an overhang, step edge, negative obstacle, or narrow structure. The traversal policy combines local map crops with proprioception and a desired direction or velocity, then outputs body or joint-level commands. Privileged simulation geometry can train a teacher; the deployable student must tolerate partial, noisy maps from onboard depth or LiDAR. Domain randomization and terrain curriculum cover height, slope, friction, and gaps.

Safety remains statistical. A missing return at a cliff may look like unknown rather than occupied, while map fusion can smear thin supports and latency can move a foothold beyond its assumed cell. Evaluation should split falls, collisions, slips, and route failures and test sensor dropout, pose drift, transparent/dark surfaces, and changed payload. Unknown cells should carry explicit risk instead of being inpainted automatically. A short-horizon foothold or support-polygon verifier could reject unsafe learned commands, and predictive map layers could account for body motion during sensing rather than treating the grid as instantaneous.

### Layer semantics, teacher supervision, and controller-aware traversability

Multiple elevation hypotheses per `(x,y)` cell allow the mapper to retain floor plus ceiling, the top and bottom of a step, or surfaces observed from different angles. The policy or planner still needs layer semantics: which surface is support, which is an obstacle, which is occluded, and which is stale? Local map patches should carry height, variance, normal, observation age, and validity for each layer. Fusing them with base pose, velocity, joint state, contacts, and navigation command gives the policy enough context to select a gait and body clearance.

Privileged training can supply exact terrain and contacts to a teacher or critic, while a student learns from reconstructed layers. The key ablation is the teacher–student gap under height noise, missing returns, pose drift, and latency. Comparisons with single elevation and voxels should match resolution, range, parameter count, and compute. Terrain success must be broken down by overhang, negative obstacle, stairs, narrow supports, vegetation, and transitions; an average score hides the reason for using multiple layers.

Traversability is controller-dependent. A geometric surface may be safe at low speed but not with a particular gait, payload, or approach direction. A useful extension is to predict command-conditioned failure probability and support quality, rather than convert layers into one static cost. Unknown regions should slow exploration instead of being treated as obstacles forever or free space optimistically. A model-predictive support verifier can check the learned command against reachable footholds and body clearance. This hybrid retains learning's adaptability while making the final safety assumptions visible.

## Learning Robust Autonomous Navigation and Locomotion for Wheeled-Legged Robots

This system integrates urban navigation with a wheeled-legged robot’s ability to roll efficiently and step over obstacles. Exteroceptive terrain information, proprioception, and goal commands feed a learned locomotion controller, while mapping and planning select safe paths through static and dynamic environments. Training uses privileged simulation signals, noise/randomization, and a student suitable for onboard deployment. The key result is reliable outdoor autonomy that exploits both wheels and legs instead of treating locomotion as a fixed base. Complexity lies in the full stack: perception, planner, learned controller, and hardware all introduce failure modes, and semantic goal selection is not the central contribution.

### Two-policy hierarchy and kilometer-scale deployment

The learned stack contains two RL policies at different rates. The low-level controller (LLC), an RNN-based locomotion policy trained with privileged learning, runs at 50 Hz. At deployment it relies on raw IMU and joint-encoder measurements, maintains a recurrent belief state that implicitly captures terrain and disturbances, and outputs leg joint-position and wheel-velocity commands. The high-level controller (HLC) runs at 10 Hz and directly outputs desired body velocity for the LLC, replacing the usual local path-follower and velocity-planning layers.

HLC inputs are deliberately richer than a single waypoint: two robot-relative waypoints, local terrain heights/elevation data with a safety margin, the LLC hidden state, and 20 previously visited positions with timestamps sampled at roughly 0.5 m intervals (about 10 m of route history). The recurrent low-level belief gives HLC a compact estimate of current mobility; visitation history lets it recognize oscillation and backtrack around obstacles. Training worlds are procedurally generated with Wave Function Collapse and annotated with navigation graphs so episodes contain solvable but varied detours, narrow passages, rough terrain, and dynamic obstacles. Reward favors reaching the goal through short paths rather than merely surviving random layouts.

The real system surrounds these policies with substantial classical infrastructure: multiple LiDARs, a forward RGB stereo camera, elevation mapping, person detection, localization against a pre-scanned point cloud, a human-authored topological graph encoding routes/social exclusions, and a global shortest-path query. The strongest evidence is therefore not “fully end-to-end autonomy,” but robust integration: an 8.3-km urban mission and operation amid stairs, curbs, poles, and pedestrians while exploiting wheels for efficiency.

The separation is insightful because HLC plans in the capability representation already learned by LLC, rather than assuming every geometrically free path is equally traversable. Yet the pre-scan and manually prepared graph are significant deployment costs, and failures can arise from localization, unseen drop-offs, glass, people, or belief-state drift. Future work should learn/upkeep the global graph online, attach calibrated risk to HLC commands, expose interpretable terrain affordances instead of only a hidden state, and evaluate recovery/intervention statistics across weather, crowds, and much longer unattended missions.

### Multi-rate hierarchy and wheeled-legged specificity

The high-level controller receives upcoming waypoints and a safety-expanded local height map and emits desired planar/body velocity at about 10 Hz. A recurrent low-level RL policy combines that command with proprioceptive history and terrain information, producing wheel and leg commands at 50 Hz. Recurrence helps infer slip, contact, and unmodeled dynamics. LiDAR supplies geometry and localization, while front stereo/RGB perception supports near-field hazards; a separate safety layer can override commands rather than expecting reward shaping to prevent every collision.

Wheeled legs create choices absent from ordinary quadrupeds: rolling is efficient on smooth ground, stepping clears discontinuities, and leg posture changes clearance or stability. A successful controller must learn when to transition without abrupt commands or wheel spin. Field tests are valuable because mud, vegetation, glare, latency, and calibration are difficult to randomize faithfully. Evaluation should report energy per distance, wheel slip, leg impacts, safety overrides, tracking delay, and performance per terrain class. Explicit uncertainty and a recovery mode for localization loss or trapped wheels would strengthen the hierarchy; high-level planning should also reason about energy and transition feasibility rather than treating all traversable cells equally.

### Training signals, recurrent low-level control, and field evidence

The 10-Hz high-level actor converts two route waypoints and a safety-adjusted height map into a desired velocity, allowing it to anticipate turns rather than chase a single point. The 50-Hz recurrent low-level controller receives this command with proprioception and recent experience, then coordinates wheels and legs. Recurrence is particularly appropriate for wheel slip and contact because these latent conditions are inferred from how commanded motion differs from measured motion. Privileged simulation state can train the critic or teacher, while deployment uses onboard maps and state estimation.

Reward design should expose competing objectives: waypoint progress and heading, velocity tracking, body stability, collision, wheel slip, leg clearance, torque/power, action rate, and termination. A safety margin in the height map is a representation-level prior, while the independent safety layer is a runtime constraint; these should be ablated separately. Field trials are convincing only when route length, autonomy, interventions, lighting/weather, terrain type, and perception/localization failures are reported, not just curated successes.

The hierarchy fits wheeled-legged hardware because it separates route cadence from rapid hybrid locomotion. However, a velocity command can hide whether the high level understands an upcoming step or merely relies on low-level recovery. Visualizing command changes and gait/wheel transitions around obstacles would clarify learned strategy. Future work should make terrain mode and feasibility explicit, route around hazards that waste energy or exceed wheel/leg capability, and expose recurrent-state uncertainty. When mapping or localization becomes unreliable, a safe degradation policy—slow, stop, or backtrack—matters more than nominal tracking accuracy.

## Learning to Evolve: Multi-modal Interactive Fields for Robust Humanoid Navigation in Dynamic Environments

This paper targets manipulation-oriented navigation when objects move and a once-valid map becomes stale. It maintains three coupled representations: an appearance field for view synthesis and semantic grounding, a spatial field expressed as a dynamically updated topological scene graph, and a geometry field producing watertight meshes for interaction-pose safety. The system can detect relocation, revise graph relations, and replan to safe manipulation viewpoints. The novel idea is an evolving field-based memory covering appearance, topology, and contact geometry. Its sophistication also creates substantial mapping and compute dependencies; errors can propagate across the coupled fields.

### Interactive fields, adaptation loop, and terminal-pose reasoning

The system addresses a more demanding problem than reaching an object category: a humanoid must navigate to a pose from which it can safely manipulate a particular object in an office that may have changed since mapping. Egocentric RGB-D observations feed three representations with distinct roles. The **appearance field** is a semantic 3-D Gaussian-splatting map. It uses uncertainty or reliability gating so images degraded by gait-induced shake do not corrupt the radiance/semantic model. The **spatial field** converts stabilized rendered views into a topological scene graph whose nodes and relations support language-level search. A vision-language model detects semantic or relational discrepancies—such as an object no longer being on its recorded table—and triggers map correction, relocalization, and replanning rather than continuing with stale memory.

Near the goal, the **geometry field** changes resolution and purpose. Active view selection collects task-local evidence around the target. A flow-matching Transformer then reconstructs a target-centered, watertight surface rather than relying only on sparse depth or visually attractive Gaussian primitives. The resulting mesh supports Interaction Pose Safety checks covering collision, reachability, and balance-aware placement of the humanoid before manipulation. The outputs of the overall pipeline are therefore updated semantic/topological goals, a navigation route, and a verified terminal interaction pose; it is not one monolithic policy mapping images to joints.

The paper's most distinctive point is that environmental memory is treated as something that must evolve. Many semantic navigation systems can re-observe an object, but they do not explicitly reconcile appearance, relational topology, and manipulation geometry after changes. Separating those fields also prevents a representation optimized for view synthesis from being mistaken for collision-quality geometry.

That decomposition is powerful but creates a long causal chain: uncertain RGB-D data affect Gaussian reconstruction; the VLM decides whether a discrepancy is real; graph edits affect route choice; and generative surface completion affects the terminal safety test. A watertight predicted mesh is not necessarily an accurate mesh, so IPS is a model-conditioned check rather than a formal certificate. Useful extensions include temporal change probabilities instead of binary replacement, uncertainty propagation from sensing through IPS, explicit dynamic-human modeling, compute/latency profiling on the robot, and active views chosen for safety-critical geometry rather than generic reconstruction quality.

### Three fields and adaptation to scene change

The multi-modal interactive field is not one monolithic neural map. An appearance field distills visual/semantic features from noisy egocentric RGB-D observations; a semantic or topological field stores object and region relationships for target reasoning; and a geometry field maintains object-centered shape or mesh information needed for physically meaningful arrival. Feature distillation makes online querying cheaper than repeatedly running the largest encoders, while mismatch detection decides when moved, removed, or newly observed objects should revise memory.

This division addresses humanoid-specific camera oscillation: raw frame fusion can blur geometry and semantics when the torso moves abruptly. It also changes success from reaching a coordinate to reaching a target with an appropriate spatial relation and usable geometry. The risk is correlated error across fields—a bad pose can distort appearance, create a duplicate graph node, and deform recovered geometry simultaneously. Evaluation should include relocation/removal events, map-update latency, false change detection, target-centered final pose, and task success after long occlusion. Pose-uncertainty-aware fusion, explicit object identity hypotheses, and active revisiting would improve robustness; dynamic people should be modeled as trajectories rather than repeatedly inserted into a nominally static object field.

### Field update rules, object geometry, and decisive evaluation

The appearance field must fuse semantic features without averaging together motion blur, occlusion, and viewpoint-dependent appearance. Confidence-weighted feature distillation can preserve stable object identity while reducing online encoder cost. The semantic field maintains nodes and relations over time, so relocation is detected as disagreement between a new observation and stored pose rather than being appended as a duplicate. The geometry field reconstructs target-centered shape from partial views, supplying surface and approach information that a coordinate-only ObjectNav system lacks.

These fields operate at different rates: egocentric observations arrive with locomotion; feature and pose fusion update locally; graph reasoning occurs when evidence or goals change; detailed mesh recovery may be reserved for candidate targets. Reporting this schedule, memory growth, GPU usage, and end-to-end subgoal latency is necessary to judge practicality. Ablations should remove uncertainty filtering, topology, geometry completion, or change handling while keeping the same perception backbone. Static-navigation success alone cannot validate a method whose central claim is evolution.

The strongest tests deliberately move, remove, and add target or distractor objects after mapping, then measure detection latency, wrong-goal visits, final target-relative pose, collision, and recovery. Humanoid camera oscillation should be varied by gait speed and head stabilization. My judgment is that object-centric geometry is most valuable at the final approach or manipulation boundary; for distant search, coarse topology is enough. A planner should exploit that asymmetry and request expensive shape refinement only when it changes action. Maintaining multiple pose/identity hypotheses would also prevent premature map edits when an observation is ambiguous.

## MSGNav: Unleashing the Power of Multi-modal 3D Scene Graph for Zero-Shot Embodied Navigation

[MSGNav](https://arxiv.org/abs/2511.10376) preserves images as relations in a multimodal 3-D scene graph instead of compressing every observation into text. Key-subgraph selection limits VLM context, adaptive vocabulary updates support open-set targets, and closed-loop reasoning revises exploration. A visibility-based viewpoint module tackles the “last mile”: stopping where the target is actually observable and reachable. It is training-free and reports strong GOAT-Bench and HM3D-OVON results. The design is especially useful for auditable semantic memory, though online graph construction, foundation-model latency, and perception errors remain practical constraints.

### Multimodal graph and the last-mile viewpoint problem

M3DSG associates metric object/place nodes with dynamically selected image evidence on relational edges rather than reducing every relation to a generated text sentence. Given a language goal, Key Subgraph Selection extracts only relevant nodes, relations, and images for foundation-model reasoning. Adaptive Vocabulary Update adds categories encountered or requested at runtime instead of committing to the detector's original closed list. Closed-Loop Reasoning compares predicted semantic progress with new observations and can revise the exploration choice. Geometric mapping/planning then turns the selected target into motion.

The final Visibility-based Viewpoint Decision is more consequential than it may sound. Finding the correct object node is not enough: stopping behind it, too close, or across an obstruction can satisfy map distance while failing the task. The module scores candidate final poses for target visibility and physical feasibility, outputting an executable endpoint. Thus network/VLM inputs are a compact multimodal subgraph plus instruction and current state; outputs are semantic exploration targets and a final viewpoint, not low-level joint commands. The method is zero-shot, with evaluation on GOAT-Bench and HM3D-OVON.

Preserving visual evidence postpones irreversible semantic compression and lets a VLM re-inspect ambiguous relationships. Key-subgraph selection also controls context and cost. Yet graph construction still depends on pose, open-vocabulary segmentation, and instance association; retrieval can remove the correct evidence before reasoning, and dynamic images can make graph memory large. The viewpoint score is a geometric/visibility proxy rather than proof that the controller can reach and observe the target. Strong extensions include uncertainty-aware association and retrieval, time-aware movable objects, context/memory scaling studies, and end-to-end real-robot evaluation of final visibility, collision, latency, and recovery.

### Modalities, graph query, and execution

MSGNav binds several kinds of evidence to one 3-D scene graph: object category and visual embedding, metric position/extent, room or region membership, observed images, and relational edges. For a text goal, a language or multimodal model reasons over this compact graph to rank known objects or promising unexplored regions. The selected node becomes a geometric subgoal; mapping, path planning, and obstacle avoidance execute it. This is zero-shot semantic search, not an LLM directly producing wheel or joint actions.

Multimodality helps when any single cue is weak: visual similarity can distinguish instances, room context supplies commonsense priors, and geometry rules out unreachable candidates. But graph construction is itself an estimator. Pose drift duplicates nodes, detector errors assign wrong labels, and an LLM can turn uncertain edges into confident stories. Strong evaluation should ablate each modality while holding navigation fixed and report node association precision, graph update cost, model calls, and failures by perception versus reasoning. Probabilistic nodes, negative evidence, timestamps, and contradiction repair are natural extensions. Final target confirmation should use a fresh local observation rather than trusting a stale graph entry.

### Graph construction, prompt contract, and cost-aware reasoning

The graph-building front end must solve open-vocabulary detection, depth lifting, instance association, room segmentation, and edge creation. Each node should retain category distribution rather than one label, representative image embeddings, 3-D extent, observation count, and last-seen time. Metric edges allow a selected semantic node to become a navigable waypoint; relational edges support prompts such as finding an object near another object. Multimodal reasoning is useful only because these grounded attributes are compact enough for a foundation model to inspect.

Prompt design is part of the algorithm. Supplying the entire graph eventually exceeds context and adds distractors, whereas an overly aggressive summary removes rare but decisive nodes. Retrieval should first select reachable rooms, candidate categories/attributes, and frontier nodes; the model should return a structured node identifier with rationale/confidence, not free-form coordinates. A geometric validator rejects nonexistent or unreachable IDs. Call caching and event-triggered prompting reduce latency and cost.

Evaluation should hold detector, mapper, controller, and LLM fixed while comparing graph modalities and retrieval schemes. Beyond success/SPL, report association quality, prompt size, calls, wall-clock latency, object confirmation precision, and recovery from a planted false node. Scene-graph gains on simulation may partly reflect clean depth and poses; real tests need clutter, repeated instances, moved objects, and viewpoint changes. My preferred extension is Bayesian graph maintenance with negative evidence and active views selected by expected reduction in target uncertainty. This would turn LLM commonsense into one prior among measured evidence rather than the final authority.

## MEM: Multi-Modal Elevation Mapping for Robotics and Learning

MEM enriches an elevation map with aligned signals such as height, color, semantic class, surface normals, and uncertainty. A modular GPU pipeline projects multiple sensors into a common robot-centric grid and exposes the result to classical planners or learning policies. The achievement is a reusable interface between raw multimodal perception and terrain-aware navigation. Its explicit uncertainty and synchronized layers are valuable for safety and training. Like other elevation maps, it cannot fully express arbitrary 3-D occupancy, and fusion quality depends on calibration, pose estimation, and modality-specific confidence.

### Unified image/point input and layer-specific fusion

MEM extends the GPU robot-centric elevation mapper with a generic route for both point-cloud attributes and image-valued modalities. Pose and calibration project each measurement into map cells; the framework then selects a fusion rule appropriate to the data type instead of averaging everything numerically. Continuous appearance or learned features can use weighted mean/variance, class evidence can use Bayesian or class-max/average updates, and confidence or latest-observation layers can follow their own temporal semantics. Plugins derive new layers such as traversability, line detections, surface descriptors, or masks without changing the core map.

The map is therefore an explicit tensor-like interface: geometry, RGB, semantic labels, normals/features, uncertainty, and application layers share coordinates and can be consumed by classical code or neural policies. There is no single network architecture or action output; the paper demonstrates the framework across robots/sensor configurations and applications including colorization, human detection, and line detection. GPU association and fusion keep the richer representation online-capable.

The important design choice is postponing information collapse. A scalar traversability cost cannot explain whether risk comes from slope, vegetation class, low confidence, or a person; aligned layers let downstream tasks make different decisions from the same observations. But common grid coordinates do not imply statistically valid fusion. Semantic probabilities may be miscalibrated, modalities have different latency/resolution, and pose error creates systematic cross-layer ghosting. The 2.5-D surface also loses ceilings and multiple levels. Future work should model per-modality timestamps/correlation, preserve provenance, decay dynamic classes differently from static terrain, carry multi-surface geometry where necessary, and evaluate downstream decision risk—not just attractive map visualizations.

### Fusion outputs and learning interface

MEM fuses complementary terrain cues—typically geometric height from depth or LiDAR with appearance-derived semantic/material information—into aligned elevation layers. Each cell should retain measured height, uncertainty, validity/age, and modality-specific features rather than prematurely reducing everything to one traversability score. A downstream supervised or RL model can then learn that visually similar slopes differ because one is grass, water, loose soil, or a hard step, while a classical planner can still inspect the geometric layers.

Calibration and synchronization are the central engineering risks. Projecting an RGB semantic boundary into the wrong height cell can be worse than omitting it, and learned material predictions may be overconfident under lighting or seasonal shift. Missing geometry, occlusion, and dynamic objects require explicit unknown and temporal decay. Evaluation should measure traversability decisions and robot outcomes, not only map reconstruction: false-safe terrain, false-conservative detours, slip, localization perturbation, and transfer across sensors or weather. Uncertainty-aware late fusion, self-supervision from experienced slip/contact, and a 3-D fallback for overhangs would improve reliability while retaining the compact elevation interface.

### Modal uncertainty, supervision, and terrain-centric validation

Geometry and appearance should remain separable long enough to preserve their uncertainty. LiDAR or depth determines surface height and roughness; RGB segmentation or learned embeddings suggest material, vegetation, water, stairs, or semantic obstacles; proprioceptive outcomes such as slip, sinkage, impact, and tracking error provide embodiment-specific supervision. Projection into the grid uses sensor extrinsics and pose covariance, so each semantic feature should inherit spatial uncertainty rather than becoming a crisp color attached to one cell.

There are several possible consumers. A hand-designed cost layer can combine slope and category; a supervised traversability network can predict experienced cost from map patches; an RL locomotion policy can receive raw multimodal layers. Papers should state exactly which gradients train which components and whether the mapper is frozen. A model trained only from semantic labels may look intelligent while encoding no relationship to real traction or support. Self-supervision from executed motion is more directly useful but risks unsafe exploration and selection bias.

Appropriate experiments vary lighting, season, vegetation, wetness, and sensor availability while keeping geometry similar, then vary geometry while appearance is misleading. Report calibration, false-safe/false-unsafe rates, route cost, slip/fall, compute, and performance after masking each modality. Modality dropout during training and a confidence-aware fusion gate can degrade gracefully. The representation remains 2.5-D, so overhangs and stacked surfaces need a multilayer or voxel escape path. The paper's enduring value is not “more channels,” but a reusable map where learning can connect what terrain looks like to how the robot actually experiences it.

## NeuPAN: Direct Point Robot Navigation with End-to-End Model-based Learning

NeuPAN maps raw point clouds into a latent distance representation and embeds that learned perception directly inside a model-based trajectory optimization problem. A plug-and-play proximal network efficiently handles the large set of point-level collision constraints, producing controls for nonholonomic robots without building a conventional map. The contribution is an interpretable hybrid: end-to-end trainable components with explicit dynamics and optimization structure. It runs in real time and transfers across environments and platforms. As a local planner, it needs a goal or global guidance and can be limited by sensor range, moving-obstacle prediction, and model mismatch.

### Learned optimizer, point-level constraints, and control interface

NeuPAN deliberately avoids the common point-cloud-to-occupancy-grid-to-planner chain. The network receives a raw local LiDAR point set together with robot state and a local navigation objective. A neural point encoder converts obstacle samples into compact pointwise or latent distance features relative to the robot's full footprint. Those features parameterize a finite-horizon optimization containing explicit robot dynamics, predicted states/actions, goal-progress terms, and collision constraints. Thus the output is a short dynamically feasible control trajectory—typically linear/angular controls for a nonholonomic platform or the corresponding command interface for another base—not joint torques and not a purely reactive steering label.

The computational contribution is the proximal alternating-minimization network (PAN). The navigation problem is arranged into blocks whose updates resemble a solver for a biconvex, regularized trajectory problem; the updates are unrolled as trainable layers. The neural regularized motion planner can be optimized end-to-end using data and model/reward knowledge while retaining recognizable roles for dynamics, collision geometry, and objective terms. “End-to-end” here therefore means differentiable perception-through-optimization, not an opaque policy that discards modeling. The learned point representation and structured solver are jointly responsible for real-time operation with many obstacle points.

This hybrid is the highlight. Exact or close point-to-body distances preserve narrow-gap and full-shape information that coarse grids lose, while the model-based horizon discourages the shortsighted oscillations of a simple point-policy. The same structure also makes the method more portable than a policy tied to one map raster. Its guarantees remain conditional, however: a finite point cloud contains occlusions; moving obstacles require reliable temporal prediction; unmodeled slip and latency invalidate rollout dynamics; and local optimization can still enter dead ends. “Map-free” does not mean memory-free global autonomy—a route or subgoal is still needed for large environments.

Future work should expose calibrated distance uncertainty, incorporate multi-scan motion history, enforce hard emergency constraints outside the learned solver, and study solver behavior under severe distribution shift. Comparisons should report minimum clearance and intervention rate in addition to success, because average trajectory efficiency can conceal rare but safety-critical constraint violations.

### Unrolled optimizer, inputs, and guarantees

NeuPAN encodes raw LiDAR point sets into learned distance-like latent features and places them inside a model-based trajectory optimizer. Its proximal alternating-minimization network unrolls repeated updates of robot controls and obstacle-related variables, with trainable regularizers improving convergence and conservatism. The robot model, action bounds, and collision constraints remain explicit. Input is a live point cloud plus goal and robot state; output is a feasible short control sequence, of which the first command is executed in receding horizon.

This hybrid is different from both occupancy-map planning and black-box end-to-end RL. Learning compresses irregular points and supplies useful priors, while optimization preserves interpretable dynamics and constraint structure and can handle arbitrary shapes or moving points at high rate. The safety claim nevertheless depends on point coverage, state estimation, motion prediction, solver convergence, and the chosen robot footprint. Tests should report minimum clearance, constraint violation, solve-time tails, iterations, and behavior under sparse/dropout scans. A certified fallback for nonconvergence, uncertainty-inflated latent distances, multi-hypothesis dynamic prediction, and warm starts from previous solutions would make the learned optimizer safer in dense real traffic.

### Training engines, optimizer structure, and closed-loop diagnostics

NeuPAN separates a data engine, which supplies point-cloud/navigation examples, from a knowledge engine containing robot dynamics and collision constraints. The point encoder learns a latent obstacle-distance representation. The PAN architecture unrolls a fixed number of proximal alternating steps, exchanging learned features and explicit optimization variables. Trainable regularizers or step parameters can be optimized end to end through the unrolled solve, while the resulting action sequence still obeys a known robot model more directly than an unconstrained policy.

This structure offers interpretable ablations: replace learned distance with geometry, replace the learned regularizer with a classical penalty, vary unroll depth, remove dynamics constraints, or compare converged optimization with fixed-budget inference. Runtime should include point preprocessing and all iterations, with median and worst-case latency. Dynamic tests need stated prediction model and horizon; treating the latest point cloud as static can succeed only when obstacles are slow relative to replanning.

Direct-point planning avoids occupancy discretization but inherits visibility: free space behind a surface is unknown, sparse scans miss thin objects, and localization error shifts every point. A latent “distance” should be calibrated against true minimum robot-shape clearance, especially for noncircular wheeled-legged bodies. Online diagnostics should expose residuals, constraint slack, and convergence; if the unrolled network produces an infeasible sequence, a conservative stop or certified backup controller is required. Future work should propagate per-point and motion-prediction uncertainty through the optimizer and learn warm starts, while retaining explicit verification after the neural update.

## OpenGS-SLAM: Open-Set Dense Semantic SLAM with 3D Gaussian Splatting for Object-Level Scene Understanding

OpenGS-SLAM combines dense Gaussian-splatting SLAM with open-vocabulary 2-D foundation models. Explicit semantic labels are fused into 3-D Gaussians; Gaussian Voting Splatting renders label maps efficiently, while confidence-aware association reduces noisy or inconsistent 2-D predictions. The map supports object-level queries beyond a fixed training taxonomy, which is useful for language-driven navigation. Its novelty is explicit, online open-set semantics inside a dense SLAM representation. It inherits the compute and memory demands of Gaussian maps and can preserve systematic mistakes from the vision foundation model.

### RGB-D pipeline, label consensus, and open-set map output

The input is a live RGB-D sequence. RGB frames pass through an interchangeable semantic generator—YOLO-World for open-vocabulary category evidence and a full-scene segmenter such as MobileSAMv2—while generalized ICP estimates the current camera pose. Keyframes densify a 3-D Gaussian map, with G-ICP covariance used to initialize new primitives. Each Gaussian stores ordinary differentiable appearance/geometry parameters plus an explicit, non-differentiable semantic label; the output is a tracked pose and dense object-level 3-D map that can render RGB, depth, or labeled views.

Gaussian Voting Splatting is the important efficiency mechanism. For each pixel the renderer records the top 50 contributing, depth-sorted Gaussians and accumulates alpha-compositing weight by label; the label with greatest total contribution wins. This both renders a map prediction and identifies exactly which primitives should change when new evidence disagrees. Confidence-based 2-D label consensus then classifies input/map overlap as full match, partial match, whole match, or new object. Confidence uses object completeness in the current view; repeated evidence decays spurious part labels created by over-segmentation. “Counter Gaussians” inconsistent with better-supported segment boundaries can be pruned, allowing cleaner replacements.

Unlike feature-distillation methods, this design does not store a large CLIP-like vector in every primitive and needs no predefined closed class list. Its highlight is solving the mundane but central online problem that the same object is assigned different segment IDs—or split into parts—from different viewpoints. Explicit labels make queries and debugging easier and yield real-time rendering.

Open-set nevertheless should not be confused with error-free semantics. It is inherited from the 2-D foundation detectors; missed objects, category hallucinations, and similar adjacent instances can still be fused incorrectly. G-ICP assumes usable depth/geometry, and irreversible consensus may turn an early mistake into persistent map state. The Replica/TUM-style evaluation is also much shorter and cleaner than lifelong robot operation. Natural next steps include reversible data association, label/posterior uncertainty, loop-closure-aware semantic correction, memory bounds, dynamic-object handling, and task-level tests showing that object queries improve actual navigation success.

### Label pipeline, Gaussian voting, and navigation value

OpenGS-SLAM takes an RGB-D stream. An open-vocabulary recognition/segmentation front end proposes labels and masks with confidence; geometric/photometric SLAM estimates camera pose and grows a 3-D Gaussian map. Gaussian Voting Splatting projects label evidence into the map and renders it back to views, allowing repeated observations to vote for consistent object labels rather than storing a high-dimensional feature vector on every Gaussian. The outputs are camera trajectory, dense renderable geometry/appearance, semantic views, and queryable object instances.

The efficient categorical representation makes object-level mapping practical, but voting can entrench early mistakes. Closely spaced instances may merge, label synonyms fragment one object, and moving objects violate the static Gaussian assumption. Pose error contaminates both geometry and the semantic vote. Navigation should therefore consume label confidence, instance extent, and observation recency, not just a winning category. Evaluation should include open-set queries, incremental new labels, instance association, memory/FPS, and object-goal success under map error. Language-normalized labels, reversible evidence accumulation, dynamic-object layers, and active views chosen to resolve ambiguous instances are stronger next steps than simply adding more categories.

### Open-set semantics, instance maintenance, and map utility

The recognition pipeline first proposes vocabulary from image content and text-image priors, then segmentation produces per-pixel label/confidence. Gaussian Voting Splatting associates these observations with visible Gaussians and aggregates categorical evidence in 3-D; rendering the map back into the current view produces a consistent label map that can correct fragmented frame-level predictions. Because the map is dense and differentiably renderable, pose, geometry, appearance, and semantics share a spatial representation.

Open-set does not mean unlimited error-free vocabulary. Synonyms and granularity—“sofa,” “couch,” “furniture”—must be normalized; novel labels may conflict with stored votes; and per-class confidence from foundation models is poorly calibrated. Instance-level navigation additionally requires separating two objects of the same class and preserving identity across occlusion. Memory, label-update time, recognition-model latency, Gaussian count, and semantic rendering rate should be reported alongside pose and reconstruction accuracy.

The most decisive downstream experiment is ObjectNav or object retrieval using the constructed map, with failures separated into pose, geometry, category, instance association, and reachability. Static benchmark segmentation can reward large surfaces while missing small task objects. Evidence accumulation should be reversible: one incorrect early label must not dominate forever, and a moved object should detach from its static Gaussians. Language embeddings can support label merging, while timestamps and dynamic tracks handle movable instances. Active viewpoint selection should maximize expected semantic/instance clarification. This would make OpenGS-SLAM a scene-understanding memory for action rather than only an impressive renderer.

## Omni-Perception Omnidirectional Collision Avoidance for Legged Locomotion in Dynamic Environments

Omni-Perception gives a legged robot full-surround awareness for dynamic collision avoidance rather than relying on a forward camera. Multiple range views are fused into a compact representation and combined with proprioception and navigation commands in a learned locomotion policy. Training exposes the policy to moving obstacles, sensing noise, and varied approach directions. Its contribution is reactive avoidance integrated with gait control, allowing evasive whole-body behavior without stopping to replan. It is principally a local safety capability; occlusion, prediction of purposeful human motion, and global deadlock resolution need higher-level support.

### Temporal raw LiDAR and proximal–distal risk encoding

The policy directly consumes temporal raw LiDAR point clouds instead of first constructing an elevation or occupancy map. Its remaining input is a matching history of joint position/velocity, base linear/angular velocity, projected gravity, and the desired linear/yaw command. PD-RiskNet divides geometry by control urgency: proximal points receive fine, high-priority processing for imminent collision, while distal structure is represented more coarsely for anticipation. Hierarchical spatial features are fused over time and with proprioception, after which a PPO actor outputs joint-position targets.

Training jointly rewards velocity tracking and clearance while regularizing collision, gait stability, smoothness, torque, and posture. A GPU raycaster spanning Isaac Gym, Genesis, and MuJoCo supplies millions of temporal scans with range/beam noise, dropout, motion latency, and moving obstacles. Real tests include side/rear approaches, overhead, transparent, slender, and floor-level obstacles. The key insight is that control does not need uniform reconstruction fidelity: near geometry deserves resolution and immediate response, whereas distant geometry mainly establishes trend. Omnidirectional LiDAR also avoids the lighting and field-of-view weaknesses of a forward depth camera.

This remains reactive rather than predictive social navigation. A short scan history only implicitly separates self-motion from obstacle motion and can deadlock in crowds or passages. LiDAR has minimum-range, thin/dark-return, motion-distortion, and self-occlusion failure modes, and PPO provides no formal clearance guarantee. Explicit scene flow/time-to-collision, uncertainty calibration, a safety shield, and coupling to global route planning are natural next steps. Equal-budget ablations against range images and voxels would also show whether gains come from raw point processing or simply better sensor coverage.

### Sensor coverage and command hierarchy

Omnidirectional avoidance needs geometry around the full body because a legged robot can sidestep, rotate, or be approached from behind while its forward camera remains clear. Multiple depth views or LiDAR are fused into a robot-centered obstacle representation; temporal features estimate motion; a navigation layer combines goal direction with collision risk and emits a velocity or short-horizon body command. A locomotion policy then realizes that command with joint actions. The safety-relevant history is the obstacle trajectory, not merely a single scan.

The key question is whether the representation accounts for the articulated swept volume and gait latency. A safe instantaneous base velocity may still swing a leg into an obstacle, and blind zones between sensors can move with body pitch. Evaluation should vary obstacle speed, approach direction, multiplicity, sensor dropout, and robot gait, reporting minimum link clearance, collision, unnecessary stop, and return-to-route time. Probabilistic trajectory prediction and reachable-set margins can turn uncertain detections into conservative commands. The system should also distinguish static geometry, purposeful pedestrians, and projectiles; one avoidance policy is unlikely to express the correct social and physical response to all three.

### Temporal prediction, articulated clearance, and safety accounting

Omnidirectional sensing should be transformed into a body-centered frame with accurate timestamps and motion compensation. Clustering and tracking estimate each obstacle's relative pose, velocity, and uncertainty; a temporal encoder or explicit predictor extrapolates likely occupancy over the locomotion response horizon. Goal direction and this dynamic occupancy feed a planner or learned policy that outputs body velocity. The 200-Hz-scale joint controller then realizes the command, but its gait phase and leg swing envelope should influence the collision margin.

Ablations should compare forward-only perception, panoramic single-frame occupancy, panoramic temporal prediction, and oracle obstacle tracks. Obstacles must approach from all bearings and at different heights and speeds, including multiple simultaneous trajectories. Report sensor-to-command latency, prediction error by horizon, minimum distance for every body link, collision, false stop, detour, and recovery to the planned route. Average base clearance can hide a foot or shoulder strike.

The term “omni-perception” is meaningful if coverage remains reliable during pitch/roll and self-occlusion. Sensor overlap, calibration drift, and blind bands should be visualized, with a conservative slowdown when uncertainty increases. A future system should combine chance-constrained predicted occupancy with a whole-body reachable set, rather than assuming the base is a disk. Social movers and high-speed projectiles also need different objectives: yielding and legibility for a person, maximum physical clearance for an incoming object. An explicit threat classifier can select the appropriate avoidance semantics without giving up a common geometric safety layer.

## Safe-SAGE: Social-Semantic Adaptive Guidance for Safe Engagement through Laplace-Modulated Poisson Safety Functions

[Safe-SAGE](https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.05497) makes safety constraints depend on what an obstacle is, not just where it is. Fused point clouds, vision instance segmentation, and persistent tracking maintain semantic obstacles outside the current camera view. A Laplace guidance field modulates a Poisson safety function used by both MPC and control-barrier-function layers, enabling class-dependent margins and passing conventions while retaining a formal safety filter. The bridge from open-world semantics to low-level safety is the main novelty. Guarantees are conditional on perception and model assumptions; misclassification can select an inappropriate margin.

### Semantic perception and two-layer safety filtering

Multi-sensor point clouds provide geometry, vision-based instance segmentation supplies class/instance identity, and persistent tracking keeps semantic obstacles alive after they leave camera view. Each class can select a different clearance and interaction convention: a person, wall, fragile object, or robot should not induce identical behavior. A Laplace-derived guidance field supplies smooth directional flow—including multi-agent passing preference—while a Poisson safety function represents obstacle exclusion. Flux modulation combines them so semantic intent changes how the robot goes around an obstacle without deleting the underlying constraint.

The controller is layered. MPC plans a context-aware local trajectory using the semantic PSF/guidance field; a faster control-barrier-function filter modifies the requested input if necessary to keep the state in the safe set. Inputs are estimated robot state plus tracked semantic obstacle geometry/motion; output is a filtered locomotion command for the legged platform. No RL policy is essential to the core, and semantics modulate conservatism rather than directly generating joint actions.

The conceptual highlight is resolving “semantic blindness” in safety control while retaining an analytic last line of defense. Persistent tracks also avoid margins disappearing merely because a person leaves the camera view. However, rigorous guarantees are conditional on correct state, class, footprint, dynamics, and bounded tracking error. A dangerous misclassification can choose too small a margin, and social passing norms are context/culture dependent. Worst-case unknown-class handling, probabilistic margins, occlusion-aware reachable sets, detector latency/dropout tests, and explicit proofs covering perception error would substantially strengthen the safety claim.

### Safety field, semantic adaptation, and control boundary

Safe-SAGE represents obstacles and socially meaningful entities with smooth Poisson-style safety potentials. Laplace modulation changes field strength or shape using semantic context—for example, a person, doorway, restricted zone, or ordinary wall need not impose the same preferred distance. The planner follows goal guidance while respecting the combined field, producing a path or velocity command for a separate tracking controller. Thus semantic perception modifies an analytic safety representation rather than asking a language model to drive the robot directly.

Smooth fields offer continuous gradients and can be inspected, but a potential is not automatically a formal barrier certificate. Incorrect class labels change clearance policy; overlapping entities may create local minima; and social comfort depends on orientation, motion, group structure, and culture rather than category alone. Evaluation should report collision and minimum distance alongside freezing, detour, approach side, and task completion for each semantic class. Confidence-weighted modulation should revert to a conservative geometric default when recognition is uncertain. Combining the field with predictive human motion and an optimization-based safety filter would clarify which constraints are guaranteed and which are learned social preferences.

### PDE interpretation, semantic parameters, and validation strategy

Poisson-based guidance constructs a smooth scalar potential whose gradient points toward task progress while boundary conditions encode obstacles or regions. Laplace modulation changes how semantic entities influence that field—strength, spatial decay, preferred engagement zone, or directional structure—without discarding the analytic planner. Unlike summing simple radial repulsions, solving a field over free space can yield smoother global guidance around shapes, although discretization and boundary setup still determine behavior.

The semantic adapter needs an explicit parameter source. Hand-set values are interpretable but laborious; learned or language-derived values scale better but need bounds. Confidence should interpolate between semantic behavior and a conservative geometric default. For people, orientation and velocity can create anisotropic zones; groups may form a joint boundary; a doorway should encourage orderly passage rather than repel the robot. These cases test whether semantics truly changes navigation rather than merely inflating all obstacles.

Validation should include analytic toy scenes, simulation crowds, and physical trials. Measure numerical solve time, path smoothness, clearance by class, collision, social-zone violation, freezing, detour, and goal completion. Compare ordinary potential fields, cost maps, social-force planners, and safety filters at matched perception. A key failure test is misclassification: what happens when a poster is called a person or a person is missed? My judgment is that the framework is strongest as interpretable guidance inside a constrained local planner. Calling it safety requires an independent barrier or reachability check, because a smooth semantic potential remains a preference, not a proof.

## SG-Nav: Online 3D Scene Graph Prompting for LLM-based Zero-shot Object Navigation

SG-Nav constructs an online 3-D scene graph from open-vocabulary detections and spatial relations, then supplies a compact graph prompt to an LLM for zero-shot ObjectNav decisions. Frontier or waypoint planning executes the chosen exploration target, and new observations update the graph. The key idea is replacing long image histories with a structured, spatially grounded prompt that an LLM can reason over efficiently. It improves interpretability and category generalization without task-specific policy training. Dependence on object detection, graph association, and LLM commonsense can still produce hallucinated or overly confident plans.

### Graph construction, hierarchical prompting, and re-perception

Online RGB-D/pose observations produce object instances that are grouped into higher-level regions/rooms and connected by metric and semantic relationships. The graph preserves more context than a list of nearby detections: object–object, object–group, and group–room structure can express where an unseen target is plausibly located. A hierarchical chain-of-thought prompt asks the LLM to traverse this structure from coarse context toward candidate objects/frontiers. Its output is an exploration target; conventional mapping and waypoint control execute motion and then add newly observed nodes.

A re-perception mechanism revisits inconsistent graph evidence so a single detector error need not permanently determine the route. The system is zero-shot—LLM commonsense and open-vocabulary perception replace navigation-policy training—and decisions are inspectable through selected graph nodes and reasoning. Tests on MP3D, HM3D, and RoboTHOR report more than ten percentage points of success-rate improvement over prior zero-shot systems and, on MP3D, performance above compared supervised agents.

The scene graph is a useful prompt compression and persistent spatial memory, but hierarchical reasoning can amplify early structural errors: a wrong room assignment prevents fine-level search from seeing the right object. Re-perception helps only when the robot chooses to observe the disputed region, and chain-of-thought plausibility is not calibrated probability. Graph maintenance, detector/instance association, LLM latency, and repeated calls remain real costs. Future work should use probabilistic graph beliefs, active views selected to resolve ambiguity, explicit prompt-cost budgets, dynamic-object timestamps, and real-robot tests that separate semantic reasoning failures from mapping and controller failures.

### Online graph lifecycle and grounding safeguards

SG-Nav constructs its 3-D scene graph during exploration rather than assuming an oracle map. RGB/depth detections are lifted into object nodes with positions and visual evidence; room or region nodes and spatial/containment edges compress experience. The text goal and graph are serialized into an LLM prompt, which selects a likely object, region, or frontier. A metric planner turns that symbolic choice into a reachable waypoint. The LLM contributes commonsense search priors, not collision avoidance.

The online lifecycle is where most failures occur. Observations must associate with existing nodes despite viewpoint change and pose drift; missing detections are negative evidence only when an object should have been visible; moved objects require stale edges to be revised. Prompt length grows with exploration, so summarization can remove decisive clues. Evaluation should publish graph precision/recall, duplication, prompt tokens and latency, calls per episode, and success after injected map errors. Confidence-weighted nodes, timestamps, reversible merges, and reachability filtering should precede LLM selection. A fresh visual confirmation should be mandatory before declaring success.

### LLM grounding, graph compression, and reproducibility

SG-Nav's prompt must bridge symbols and metric reality. Object nodes need stable IDs, class probabilities, 3-D centroids or boxes, room membership, representative crops, and observation confidence; frontier nodes need position, reachability, and exploration value. The LLM should choose among these IDs or return a room/frontier ranking in structured form. A deterministic parser and metric planner then prevent hallucinated coordinates from reaching the controller. Online updates merge repeat observations and add newly discovered structure after every executed subgoal.

The scene graph is also a compression policy. Including every detection makes prompts long and noisy; retaining only high-confidence objects can delete the target. Experiments should vary graph pruning, hierarchy, prompt format, LLM, and call schedule while holding detector and planner fixed. Report tokens, calls, latency, navigation distance, success/SPL, node association accuracy, and final target-verification precision. Oracle graph and oracle language reasoning baselines separate mapping from commonsense value.

Zero-shot results are credible when goals, categories, layouts, and linguistic forms are not tuned to the evaluation environments. Room priors can be powerful but brittle: a washing machine may sit in a hallway, or a chair may occur everywhere. The agent should combine priors with accumulated negative evidence and revise its plan when a likely room is exhausted. A probabilistic graph and belief update would formalize this process. For real deployment, moved objects, repeated instances, pose drift, and absent targets are essential; the correct outcome for an absent goal is a calibrated failure after efficient coverage, not endless LLM-driven wandering.

These safeguards are essential for reliable unattended operation in practice.

## STATE-NAV: Stability-Aware Traversability Estimation for Bipedal Navigation on Rough Terrain

STATE-NAV learns traversability from the stability actually experienced by a biped. TravFormer, a transformer estimator, uses body-to-stance-foot angle as a self-supervised instability signal and converts elevation-map context into a stability-aware command-velocity map. TravRRT* uses the map for global planning; an uncertainty-aware MPC local planner and ALIP-based controller execute the route. The novel aspect is embodiment-specific risk learned from robot experience rather than hand-set slope thresholds. It directly connects perception to dynamic stability, but its safety is empirical and tied to the robot, controller, terrain sensing, and coverage of training experience.

### TravFormer inputs, stability supervision, and planning stack

STATE-NAV replaces hand-authored terrain cost with an empirical question: for this biped and controller, how unstable would a requested velocity be on the observed patch? A ZED 2i stereo camera provides local terrain geometry that is accumulated into an elevation representation. TravFormer encodes the terrain patch with convolutional feature extraction and uses the candidate command/velocity as a query or conditioning token in a Transformer-style predictor. Its target is a body-to-stance-foot alignment measure used as a proxy for instability. Training first minimizes prediction error; a later probabilistic head estimates both mean and dispersion so the planner can penalize uncertainty rather than treating every network output as equally trustworthy. The paper reports roughly eight hours of simulated experience for constructing this estimator.

This makes the network interface more specific than a generic traversability classifier. Input: local elevation context plus a proposed motion command and relevant robot condition. Output: predicted instability and uncertainty for that command. By evaluating admissible commands, the system derives a traversability value from the maximum velocity that keeps predicted risk below a threshold. Consequently a steep patch is not assigned one universal cost: risk changes with direction, speed, embodiment, and learned controller capability.

The estimator sits inside a classical hierarchy. TravRRT* uses the stability-aware map for global route selection. A risk-aware MPC local planner runs at about 3 Hz, while an ALIP-based locomotion/controller layer executes the chosen motion; global replanning is much slower, on the order of several seconds. This separation is a strength because it makes the learned model inspectable and lets optimization enforce local dynamics. It also clarifies that TravFormer does not output joint actions.

The key contribution is grounding traversability in experienced closed-loop stability rather than geometric slope heuristics. Yet the stability labels are inseparable from the simulator, gait, state estimator, and controller used to generate them; replacing any of these can change the meaning of “safe.” Stereo/map delay, unseen compliant terrain, and poorly calibrated predictive variance are additional hazards. Strong follow-up work would validate uncertainty calibration and threshold sensitivity, adapt online from near-falls without unsafe exploration, model temporal foothold sequences, and test whether learned risk transfers across payloads, gait speeds, and hardware wear.

### Network output calibration and controller dependence

TravFormer is queried over candidate commands, not once per terrain cell. Convolutional features encode the elevation patch; command tokens enter the Transformer decoder; the output predicts expected body-to-stance-foot instability and, in the probabilistic variant, dispersion. The planner converts these predictions into a directional speed limit or risk cost. The same rock can therefore be safe when approached slowly from one direction and unsafe laterally at high speed. TravRRT* uses this field globally, MPC refines local commands, and ALIP control owns step execution.

This is a strong embodiment-aware formulation, but its label measures one controller rather than an intrinsic terrain property. Improvements to ALIP, payload change, worn feet, or estimator bias alter the geometry-command-to-risk mapping. Predictive variance is useful only if calibrated: reliability diagrams, failure versus risk threshold, and out-of-distribution tests should accompany mean error. Online residual learning from slips could update the estimator conservatively. A future model should include short terrain and footstep sequences, because biped stability depends on accumulated momentum and alternating support, not only the current patch and command.

### Supervision construction, risk planning, and evidence needed for safety

Training labels arise from closed-loop rollouts rather than terrain annotation. At each sample, the system pairs a local elevation patch and candidate command with the observed body-to-stance-foot angle or related instability statistic. This grounds risk in the biped's actual controller and morphology. Data coverage must include successful stable behavior, near-limit recovery, and failures across directions and speeds; otherwise the predictor simply learns the commands chosen by an already conservative data policy. Roughly eight simulated hours may be sufficient for the reported setting but should be accompanied by coverage histograms.

The probabilistic head converts uncertainty into planning cost. TravRRT* can reject or slow through high-risk regions globally, while local MPC trades progress against predicted mean and uncertainty under dynamics. The risk threshold defines a safety-efficiency operating point and should be swept rather than fixed without discussion. Comparisons with slope/roughness heuristics, deterministic TravFormer, oracle rollout cost, and geometry-only learned traversability identify where embodiment-conditioned prediction helps.

Real safety evidence requires calibration and transfer analysis. Report falls or loss of balance versus predicted risk, expected calibration error, false-safe rate, uncertainty under novel rocks/compliant ground, and performance after payload, gait speed, or controller gain changes. Since the supervisory proxy is body–stance alignment, it may miss foot slip, toe collision, actuator saturation, or delayed momentum failure. A richer target could predict several failure modes and short-horizon stability trajectories. Conservative online adaptation from hardware residuals, guarded by the existing planner, would keep the map current as the robot or controller changes.

## SysNav: Multi-Level Systematic Cooperation Enables Real-World, Cross-Embodiment Object Navigation

SysNav coordinates semantic target reasoning, building-scale spatial memory, global planning, local obstacle avoidance, and embodiment-specific control rather than asking a VLM to solve everything. Different levels exchange confidence and failure feedback, allowing recovery when semantic guesses or routes fail. The system is evaluated in many real-world trials and on multiple mobile embodiments. Its contribution is systems integration for long-range ObjectNav and cross-robot reuse. Such modularity helps diagnosis, but end-to-end success remains the product of many imperfect components and the cost of mapping or VLM calls can be significant.

### Three levels, structured memory, and cross-embodiment interface

At each step SysNav receives a 360-degree RGB panorama and a 3-D LiDAR point cloud, and ultimately emits continuous linear and angular velocity commands. The high level builds a three-layer scene representation: room nodes store masks, room type, and a representative panorama; viewpoint nodes sparsely store poses, coverage, and best-view images; object nodes store open-vocabulary category, confidence, 3-D points/bounding box, representative crop, and task-requested attributes. Edges encode room connectivity, containment, visibility, and queried object-object relations. VLM calls are used selectively for room labels, task-specific attributes/relations, and deciding which room is most plausible—not for every motor action.

The mid level alternates in-room coverage with cross-room search. Candidate viewpoints are scored by newly visible free/unknown boundaries; sampled candidates are ordered by a traveling-salesperson solve inside a rolling local/global horizon. On discovering a new room, an early-stop VLM query can switch exploration immediately if the target is likely there. Otherwise room histories and graph context guide which connected room to visit next. The mid level emits waypoints. A low-level base-autonomy layer performs waypoint following, collision avoidance, and terrain analysis using embodiment-specific controllers, allowing the same semantic/planning logic to drive a Mecanum base, Unitree Go2, or G1.

This is a strong argument for systematic decomposition. VLMs operate where commonsense is useful and latency is tolerable; geometric coverage handles exhaustive search; conventional control owns rapid safety. Real hardware uses a Livox Mid-360 and Ricoh Theta Z1, with additional onboard LiDAR for legged locomotion. The paper reports 190 real-world episodes across three bodies and 8,195 simulated episodes, with a 4–5x real-world improvement over compared ObjectNav baselines.

The result depends on many interfaces: panoramic detection must align with LiDAR, rooms must be segmented sensibly, graph identity must persist, and low-level feasibility differs sharply by body. A laptop-class RTX 4090 also weakens claims of lightweight deployment. Cross-embodiment reuse is mainly above the controller, not evidence that one motion policy transfers between wheels and legs. Future work should propagate uncertainty through graph and room choices, learn recovery from contradictory observations, benchmark compute/API latency and map growth, and include dynamic people and movable objects in the persistent representation.

### Cooperation signals and cross-embodiment limits

The levels communicate more than a one-way goal. High-level reasoning reprioritizes rooms when new evidence appears; middle-level coverage reports explored viewpoints and unreachable candidates; low-level autonomy reports blocked paths or terrain infeasibility. This feedback prevents a plausible VLM guess from being retried indefinitely. Sparse viewpoint storage and room summaries bound memory, while task-specific VLM queries are reserved for attributes or relations that an open-vocabulary detector cannot resolve cheaply.

Cross-embodiment reuse occurs above robot-specific control; it does not mean Mecanum, Go2, and G1 share actuation or traversability. A doorway reachable by wheels may be unsafe for a humanoid carrying an object, so feasibility must flow upward and alter room/viewpoint costs. Evaluation should separate target discovery, room choice, route feasibility, collision, and final verification per embodiment. A common affordance interface—clearance, slope, step capability, turning radius, payload—would make reuse more principled. Onboard compute and cached language embeddings would test whether the large external GPU is necessary for performance.

### System-level experiment design and reusable abstraction

SysNav's scene representation is unusually concrete: room nodes hold semantic type, panorama and mask; viewpoint nodes store pose, visibility coverage and representative imagery; object nodes store open-vocabulary category, confidence, points or bounding box, attributes and crops. Containment, connectivity, visibility, and queried object relations form edges. This schema allows the VLM to reason at room scale without processing raw sensor history and lets deterministic coverage operate inside a room.

The rolling-horizon viewpoint planner scores newly exposed free/unknown boundaries and uses a traveling-salesperson ordering to reduce redundant motion. Early-stop reasoning after room discovery is valuable because completing geometric coverage of an obviously promising room can waste time. The low level receives waypoints and handles collision and terrain with embodiment-specific autonomy. The reported 190 physical episodes across three bodies and 8,195 simulation episodes are meaningful scale, but confidence intervals and per-environment/per-target breakdowns are needed to interpret the 4–5× improvement.

Strong ablations remove room reasoning, object relations, early stopping, viewpoint ordering, and low-level feedback. Compute should include VLM/API calls, panoramic processing, graph update and the laptop RTX 4090. Cross-embodiment tests should reuse the same high-level decisions in matched buildings while exposing different reachability constraints. The reusable abstraction should be an affordance-aware waypoint request, not a point assumed feasible for all robots. If a G1 cannot cross clutter that a wheeled base can, failure feedback must update the planner instead of repeatedly retrying. This interface is the paper's best systems contribution and should be standardized and measured explicitly.

## TANGO: Humanoid Navigation in Cluttered Environments with a Whole-Body Vision-Language-Action Model

[TANGO](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.09158) maps a natural-language instruction plus egocentric RGB directly to 29-DoF humanoid action targets, treating arms, torso, and gait as part of navigation. Its simulation data pipeline combines global planning, kinematic whole-body motion generation, obstacle-aware editing, and RL tracking to create feasible supervision. This is novel because it learns language-conditioned whole-body traversal rather than issuing planar waypoints to a fixed locomotion policy. It transfers zero-shot to a Unitree G1 in reported tests. The direct policy may be difficult to verify, and RGB-only partial observability or instructions outside the synthetic distribution remain important risks.

### VLA interface and synthetic whole-body supervision

TANGO receives an egocentric RGB observation plus a natural-language route/traversal instruction and directly predicts 29-DoF joint-space action targets for downstream G1 whole-body control. Arms, torso, and gait are therefore action dimensions rather than fixed posture around a planar base. The vision-language-action model must learn that wording and scene geometry imply coordinated behaviors such as narrowing shoulders, moving an arm past clutter, rotating the torso, or changing gait—not just choosing forward/turn waypoints.

Because large human demonstrations of collision-free humanoid navigation do not exist, supervision is synthesized in four stages. A global planner selects a collision-free route; kinematic whole-body generation produces body motion along it; obstacle-aware editing adjusts links to actual 3-D clearance; and an RL tracker converts those references into dynamically executable trajectories. Rendered egocentric images, instructions, and tracked 29-joint targets then train the VLA entirely in simulation. This pipeline is as important as the model: it transforms geometric paths into action labels that respect the morphology and dynamics.

The highlight is collapsing the traditional gap between semantic navigation and whole-body clearance. Modular baselines can choose the right corridor but still strike it with an elbow; TANGO learns visual-language-conditioned posture continuously and reports strong simulation results plus zero-shot transfer to a physical G1. The same directness makes assurance difficult. Monocular RGB hides metric depth and occluded geometry, and synthetic language/motion correlations may shortcut true grounding. Joint prediction also provides no explicit collision certificate. Recurrent multi-view memory, depth/uncertainty fusion, an independent swept-volume safety filter, intervention/clearance metrics, and tests on compositional instructions and moving obstacles are necessary next steps.

### Action semantics, temporal context, and independent safety

Although described as a whole-body VLA, the model predicts desired 29-joint targets rather than raw torque. A downstream controller and servo loop realize those targets, giving stabilization and limiting authority. The model must infer metric clearance and motion phase from egocentric RGB plus language; with only a short context, an obstacle leaving view or a passage behind the camera becomes unobservable. Training data generated by planning, motion editing, and RL tracking implicitly teaches the action distribution of the specific robot and controller.

The highlight is embodiment-level supervision: language about passing sideways or keeping an arm clear maps to coordinated posture, not a planar symbol. But imitation can exploit synthetic correlations among wording, appearance, and standard trajectories. Tests should paraphrase instructions, alter textures and obstacle shapes, reverse layouts, and measure link clearance, collision impulse, joint saturation, and intervention. Depth or multi-view memory would reduce monocular ambiguity. An independent swept-volume checker should filter proposed joint chunks, while uncertainty can slow the robot when instruction or scene lies outside training support.

### Dataset synthesis, VLA architecture questions, and evidence standards

The synthetic pipeline first creates a collision-free base route, then expands it into articulated kinematic motion whose shoulders, elbows, torso, and legs respect scene geometry. Obstacle-aware editing corrects link clearance, and an RL tracker executes or rejects the reference under dynamics. Only successful tracked trajectories should become paired egocentric images, instruction text, and 29-joint targets. This acceptance step prevents the VLA from imitating geometrically attractive but dynamically impossible poses, although it can also censor difficult behaviors and narrow diversity.

Architectural details are essential for interpretation: visual backbone and image resolution, language encoder, number and duration of frames, whether action targets are single-step or chunks, causal memory, fusion mechanism, action normalization, and control rate. A Transformer with temporal tokens can remember occluded obstacles and infer motion phase; a single-image policy cannot. The downstream joint controller's gains and limits determine how much “control” belongs to the VLA versus stabilization. These should be documented with end-to-end latency.

Evaluation should compare language-conditioned whole-body action with waypoint-plus-tracker, RGB-only ablations, oracle depth/scene geometry, and motion generation without RL feasibility filtering. Measure instruction completion, path efficiency, link-wise clearance, collision, fall, action smoothness, and generalization across paraphrases, obstacle geometry, textures, and layouts. Zero-shot real G1 demonstrations are promising but require repeated trials and interventions. The key risk is shortcut learning from synthetic correlations. Counterfactual scenes where the same instruction demands a different posture, plus an external collision shield, would provide much stronger evidence of genuine language-grounded spatial control.

## UEREBot: Learning Safe Quadrupedal Locomotion under Unstructured Environments and High-Speed Dynamic Obstacles

UEREBot trains a quadruped to keep making goal progress over uneven terrain while responding to fast attacks and moving obstacles. A perception-conditioned policy combines local environment observations, robot state, and reference direction; curriculum learning and randomized dynamic disturbances develop both traversal and reflexive avoidance. The contribution is evaluating safety under short reaction times—including pushes, kicks, and other robots—rather than only slow pedestrian avoidance. The learned reactions are compelling, but they offer empirical robustness rather than a formal collision guarantee and may not generalize to unseen obstacle dynamics.

### Multi-rate planner, dual RL policies, and safety shield

UEREBot is not a single perception-to-action policy. Camera (30 Hz), LiDAR (10 Hz), robot state (50 Hz), and an offline 2.5-D terrain/static-obstacle map feed a learned spatial-temporal planner. Its shared encoder has three heads: a reference path updated at 10–20 Hz, one-step dynamic-obstacle predictions at 50 Hz, and a 50-Hz scalar threat score. A navigation RL policy consumes robot state plus the local reference segment and outputs a body-frame velocity command `[vx, vy, yaw_rate]`. In parallel, a reflex RL policy receives robot state and the predicted state of the obstacle with shortest reaction time and outputs its own evasive velocity command.

A learned threat-aware handoff continuously blends the two commands rather than switching abruptly, which is important because command discontinuities can destabilize a gait. The reflex reward prioritizes dynamic clearance, then regularizes motion/energy and recovery to stable stance; the navigation policy rewards route tracking and well-behaved progress over uneven ground. Finally, a control-barrier-function filter projects the fused command into an instantaneous safe set defined by static passability, predicted dynamic clearance, and actuation limits. Only this filtered command reaches the 200-Hz locomotion controller.

The standout contribution is reconciliation of three time scales: long-horizon goal progress, learned low-latency evasion, and last-line constraint enforcement. Many systems either replan too slowly for an incoming object or evade successfully but lose the route. Here the planner supplies intent and threat context without pretending to solve the full nonconvex optimal-control problem online. The CBF is also an important admission that soft RL rewards alone cannot enforce constraints at every step.

The “hard” safety claim is still conditional on correct obstacle state/prediction, map registration, simplified robot footprint, and CBF dynamics. Selecting one most-threatening obstacle can miss coordinated hazards, while one-step prediction is weak for purposeful motion. The offline terrain map limits deployment in changing scenes. Valuable extensions are chance-constrained multi-obstacle prediction, uncertainty-aware barrier margins, online terrain mapping, explicit camera/LiDAR dropout tests, and reporting the shield intervention distribution—frequent intervention would reveal that the learned policies themselves have not internalized safety.

### Command arbitration and conditional safety guarantees

Navigation and reflex policies operate in the same body-frame velocity space, allowing the threat-aware handoff to blend them without switching gait networks. The spatial-temporal planner supplies reference path, predicted obstacle state, and threat score; the reflex branch focuses on the shortest reaction-time obstacle; the CBF projects blended velocity into its estimated safe set before 200-Hz locomotion. This common interface permits rapid evasion followed by route reacquisition.

Every guarantee is conditional on perception and simplified barrier dynamics. A 30-Hz camera and 10-Hz LiDAR can miss a small fast object, and a 50-Hz prediction can be stale before contact. Selecting one threat compresses multi-obstacle interactions, while a safe base command may not protect swinging legs. The paper should expose CBF correction and infeasibility distributions, link clearance, detection-to-command latency, and success versus obstacle speed. Chance-constrained barriers, several simultaneous hypotheses, and a safe-stop or brace behavior for unavoidable impact are important extensions. Frequent shield intervention should be evidence that policy training needs improvement, not hidden inside final success.

### Reward separation, barrier assumptions, and high-speed test matrix

The navigation policy rewards progress along the reference path, heading or velocity agreement, and smooth energy-efficient motion over terrain. The reflex policy is trained on rapid obstacle encounters, emphasizing time-to-collision, clearance, body stability, and return to a recoverable state. Threat-aware blending must be smooth so the low-level gait does not receive a discontinuous lateral or yaw command. The CBF solves a small projection: stay as close as possible to the fused request while satisfying static passability, predicted dynamic separation, and actuation bounds.

This decomposition makes informative ablations possible: navigation alone, reflex alone, hard switching, learned blending without CBF, CBF without reflex, oracle obstacle prediction, and the full system. Testing should cross terrain difficulty with obstacle speed, size, direction, count, and sensor visibility. Report route completion, collision and link clearance, fall, unnecessary evasion, CBF intervention magnitude/frequency, infeasible safety problems, detection-to-action latency, and time to resume the path. A success metric that omits frozen or wildly detouring behavior rewards trivial conservatism.

Barrier guarantees depend on relative-degree model, obstacle prediction, sensing confidence, and command-to-body response. If the shield constrains a simplified footprint but a leg swings outside it, the mathematical guarantee does not cover the physical robot. The next version should use gait-phase-dependent whole-body envelopes and chance constraints over multiple predicted trajectories. For an unavoidable high-speed impact, no feasible velocity may exist; bracing, lowering the body, or choosing a lower-energy contact is then the correct objective. Explicitly representing this infeasible regime would make the safety claim more honest and operationally useful.

## VLFM: Vision-Language Frontier Maps for Zero-Shot Semantic Navigation

VLFM performs training-free ObjectNav by attaching vision-language relevance values to an exploration map. A pretrained image-language model scores observations for the requested object, these values are projected into a map, and a frontier policy balances semantic promise with geometric exploration. Conventional mapping and collision avoidance handle execution. The elegant contribution is turning foundation-model similarity into a spatial search heuristic without collecting navigation training data. It generalizes to open-vocabulary goals and has real-robot demonstrations, but inherits VLM biases, can chase visually correlated distractors, and uses a mostly local view of semantic evidence.

### Occupancy frontiers plus language-grounded value map

Depth observations build a metric occupancy map and expose frontiers between explored free space and unknown space. In parallel, RGB views are scored against the text goal by a pretrained vision-language model; directional/local image relevance is projected into a persistent value map. Candidate frontiers inherit semantic promise from nearby values, and a policy selects the next frontier by balancing that promise with geometric exploration. Once the target is detected, ordinary local planning/collision avoidance approaches and stops near it. Thus the foundation model outputs semantic relevance, not wheel or leg commands, and no task-specific navigation policy is trained.

This division is elegant because geometry answers “where can I move?” while Internet-pretrained semantics answers “where is the object likely to be?” Values persist spatially rather than vanishing with the current camera view. VLFM reports leading zero-shot SPL on Gibson, HM3D, and MP3D and demonstrates office ObjectNav on Boston Dynamics Spot without prior environment knowledge.

Similarity is not probability or object verification. A model can favor a kitchen-looking area for “microwave,” chase posters/reflections, or miss an unusual instance; projecting scores through imperfect pose/depth can create durable false hotspots. Frontier selection is also myopic compared with room/topological reasoning and can revisit local maxima. Calibrated open-vocabulary detection, negative evidence and temporal decay, uncertainty-aware map fusion, history/topological memory, and explicit final-view reachability would improve robustness. Reporting VLM inference cost and semantic-versus-geometric failure breakdowns is essential for understanding real deployment gains.

### Score projection and failure attribution

VLFM samples or partitions the camera view, computes text–image similarity for the goal, and projects directional scores through depth and pose into a persistent map. Frontier utility combines geometric factors such as distance or unexplored area with nearby semantic value. The chosen frontier becomes a waypoint executed by an ordinary navigation stack. This clean boundary means semantic scoring can be replaced without retraining locomotion and makes the zero-shot claim precise.

Persistent value is useful but needs negative evidence and age. One mistaken high score can attract repeated visits; failing to see a small object from one angle should not erase a room. Similarity distributions also vary by category. Evaluation should separate target detection, semantic frontier choice, mapping/localization, and control and report inference cost. Calibration, view-quality weighting, temporal decay, room priors, and final detector verification would reduce false arrival. Real-robot tests should include correlated distractors and goals absent from the building.

Frontier scores should also be normalized for travel cost and visibility, so a semantically attractive but occluded or unreachable cell cannot dominate a nearby informative observation indefinitely.

### Component choices, map update, and meaningful zero-shot ablations

VLFM's semantic score generally comes from a pretrained image–text encoder, while an open-vocabulary detector or segmentation model can verify a target. Depth builds obstacle/free-space geometry and allows each image sector's similarity to be projected into world coordinates using the current camera pose. A frontier detector extracts free/unknown boundaries; frontier utility blends semantic value, distance and exploration terms; a classical shortest-path/local controller executes the selected point. No navigation-policy gradient is required, which is the substance of “zero shot.”

Projection choices matter. Scores can be max-, mean-, or confidence-fused over views; negative observations can decay a cell; pose uncertainty can blur evidence spatially; and view frustum/occlusion determines whether absence is informative. Without these rules, persistent maps can become repositories of early mistakes. The final stop condition must distinguish seeing a target from merely entering a semantically plausible area and verify that the object is reachable from the robot's final pose.

Fair ablations hold geometry and control constant while replacing vision-language values with distance-only frontier exploration, room priors, detector confidence, or oracle target direction. Report success, SPL, distance until first target view, false detections, frontier revisits, inference time and map memory per goal category. Separate common Internet-like objects from visually unusual instances and include distractors with strong contextual similarity. The core idea is elegant and reusable, but similarity is a heuristic utility rather than probability. Calibrating it from task-agnostic data or maintaining category-specific score distributions would make frontier ranking substantially more reliable without giving up open vocabulary.

## WMNav: Integrating Vision-Language Models into World Models for Object Goal Navigation

[WMNav](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.02247) wraps VLM reasoning in a modular world-model loop that predicts decision outcomes and compares them with later observations. An online Curiosity Value Map stores predicted/explored state, and a two-stage proposer first explores broadly then localizes the target precisely. Feedback between imagined and observed states is intended to reduce hallucination and wasteful interaction. Reported HM3D and MP3D results improve success and exploration efficiency over prior zero-shot agents. Prediction is still semantic and approximate rather than a physics simulator, so errors in VLM judgments, mapping, or pose can accumulate.

### Predict–observe feedback and two-stage action proposal

WMNav decomposes a VLM agent into a world-model module, memory, action proposer, and feedback loop. From the goal, mapped observations, and candidate navigation decisions, the world model predicts likely semantic outcomes before physical execution. The online Curiosity Value Map records where the agent expects useful information and what has actually been explored. After motion, the new view is compared with the predicted outcome; disagreement feeds back into memory and the next policy decision, reducing reliance on a single hallucinated VLM answer.

Action proposal changes by search phase. Broad exploration ranks frontiers/regions likely to reveal the category; after credible target evidence appears, precise localization proposes a final approach rather than continuing coarse exploration. Conventional map/planner/controller components execute the selected target, so the VLM/world model emits semantic values and candidate decisions, not motor torques. On HM3D, reported absolute gains are +3.2% success and +3.2% SPL; on MP3D they are +13.5% success and +1.1% SPL over compared zero-shot methods.

The useful novelty is treating prediction error as navigation evidence. Many prompted agents make a choice but never ask whether the world changed as expected; WMNav closes that epistemic loop and makes memory action-dependent. Still, its “world model” is a VLM-based semantic forecast, not learned metric dynamics, and both forecast and observation interpretation may share the same bias. Curiosity values can become self-reinforcing under pose/map errors. Multiple-model uncertainty, independent target verification, counterfactual candidate scoring, longer-horizon/topological memory, and real-robot latency/failure analysis would clarify when imagined feedback genuinely reduces costly interaction.

### Internal state, prediction error, and useful ablations

The Curiosity Value Map should be read as an action-conditioned belief over where useful semantic evidence may appear. Before execution, the world model predicts what a candidate frontier or viewpoint is likely to reveal; after the geometric controller moves, perception records the actual view and updates value according to novelty and prediction mismatch. Broad exploration and target-localization proposal stages use this shared state differently. The output of the learned reasoning layer is a spatial subgoal, while mapping and local control remain responsible for reachability and collision avoidance.

A closed loop does not guarantee independent correction when the same VLM produces both expectation and interpretation. A kitchen-biased model can predict a microwave, interpret an ambiguous rectangle as supporting evidence, and reinforce its own error. Critical ablations should replace predicted outcomes with random or heuristic curiosity, use an independent detector for feedback, remove prediction-error updates, and equalize VLM calls. Useful measurements include calibration of predicted discovery, revisits, distance and calls before target evidence, false localization, and map-error sensitivity. Maintaining multiple semantic hypotheses and selecting views by expected information gain would make the “world model” closer to a belief updater than a confident narrative generator.

### Planning horizon, world-model semantics, and stronger tests

WMNav's “world model” predicts semantic observation outcomes of navigation choices, not future robot physics. This distinction determines its natural horizon: it can compare whether entering a kitchen or opening a new sightline is likely to reveal a target, while collision and dynamics remain with the metric planner. The Curiosity Value Map combines prior promise, visited state, expected novelty, and subsequent prediction error. A two-stage proposer first selects regions/frontiers for discovery and later chooses an approach viewpoint once target evidence crosses a threshold.

The feedback loop should store both confirmation and falsification. If a predicted object is absent from a high-visibility viewpoint, its local belief should decrease; if visibility was poor, uncertainty should remain. Pose error and detector miss probability must therefore enter the update. Multiple imagined candidates should be scored before physical movement, with compute cost included. Otherwise a method may improve SPL only by making more expensive model calls.

The reported HM3D and MP3D gains differ substantially, so dataset-specific scene priors and baseline saturation deserve analysis. Results should break down target frequency, room ambiguity, episode length, absent or occluded objects, and map quality. Compare semantic prediction with learned co-occurrence, LLM room priors, frontier-only exploration, and an oracle, all using the same controller. For real robots, report VLM latency, network dependence, energy/time per decision, and recovery after a wrong prediction. My assessment is that prediction–observation comparison is a real conceptual advance, but independent perception and calibrated belief updates are required before it deserves the stronger connotation of a dependable world model.
